# Coverage, Spaced Repetition, and Microlearning: Evidence Brief

**Prepared 2026-08-29.** Evidence strength is tagged **[strong]** (replicated, meta-analytic, or large-N), **[mixed]** (real but conditional or contested), **[anecdotal]** (single small study, industry data, product practice). Items marked **[unverified this session]** are well-known findings whose primary text could not be fetched here (publisher paywall/robots); treat the specific numbers as "check before quoting."

## 1. Vocabulary coverage: what the "90-10 rule" actually is

### The numbers

**Zipf's law.** Word frequencies in natural text follow a power law (rank-frequency exponent ≈ 1, equivalently exponent ≈ 2 for the frequency distribution; a large-scale analysis of English texts found the exponent clustering at 2.03 ± 0.15) ([Moreno-Sánchez et al. 2016, PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0147073)) **[strong]**. The practical consequence, widely reported from corpus counts: the top ~100 English word forms cover about 50% of running text, and the top ~1,000 cover roughly 80% ([Conti 2025, summarising corpus figures](https://gianfrancoconti.com/2025/04/14/zipfs-law-and-what-it-means-for-vocabulary-teaching-in-isla/)) **[mixed — secondary summary; exact figure varies by corpus and by whether you count word forms, lemmas or families]**.

**Nation (2006), the canonical coverage study** ([full text PDF](https://www.scienceguide.nl/wp-content/uploads/2017/11/nation-2006-vocabulary.pdf)) **[strong]**, using BNC-based word-family lists:

| Text type | 2,000 families | +1,000–2,000 more (+ proper nouns) | 98% reached at |
|---|---|---|---|
| Novels (5 combined) | 87.83% | 4,000 + PN = 94.8% | 9,000 + PN = 98.24% |
| Newspapers | ~83% | 4,000 + PN ≈ 95% | 8,000 + PN ≈ 98% |
| Spoken: conversation | 89.35% | 3,000 + PN = 96.03% | 6,000 + PN = 97.67%; 7,000 + PN = 97.95% |
| Spoken: talk-back radio | 89.41% | 3,000 + PN = 96.52% | 6,000 + PN = 98.26% |

Nation's conclusion: "If we take 98% as the ideal coverage, a 8,000–9,000 word-family vocabulary is needed for dealing with written text, and 6,000–7,000 families for dealing with spoken text." Proper nouns add 2–6% in written text and 1–1.5% in speech. 95% coverage means one unknown word in 20; 98% means one in 50.

**Spoken discourse specifically.** Adolphs & Schmitt (2003), using the CANCODE spoken corpus, found that 2,000 word families gave *less than* 95% coverage, and that ~5,000 individual words were required for ~96% coverage — overturning Schonell et al.'s 1956 claim that 2,000 families covered ~99% of speech ([Applied Linguistics abstract](https://academic.oup.com/applij/article-abstract/24/4/425/213596)) **[strong]**.

**Coverage → comprehension.**
- Laufer (1989): more learners achieved ≥55% reading comprehension at 95% coverage than below it — origin of the "95% threshold" ([summarised in Cheng & Matthews' ERIC review](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1316858.pdf)) **[mixed — small, older study]**.
- Hu & Nation (2000): at 80% coverage no learner reached adequate comprehension; at 90% few; at 95% the majority still did not; at 100%, 15/17 (multiple choice) and 10/17 (written recall) did — the source of the 98% recommendation ([same review](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1316858.pdf)) **[mixed — n=66, fiction only]**. A 2023 replication exists ([Kremmel et al., Language Learning](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/lang.12622)) **[unverified this session]**.
- Schmitt, Jiang & Grabe (2011), 661 learners in 8 countries: comprehension rises *linearly* with coverage from 90% to 100% with **no threshold jump**; 98% is "a more reasonable coverage target" for academic reading ([ERIC abstract](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ928357)) **[strong]**.
- Listening is more forgiving: van Zeeland & Schmitt (2013) found most native and non-native listeners adequately comprehended informal narratives at 90% coverage, with much less variance at 95%; they estimate 95% spoken coverage at ~2,000–3,000 word families ([Applied Linguistics abstract](https://academic.oup.com/applij/article-abstract/34/4/457/199564)) **[strong]**. Their listening means were 7.35/10 at 90%, 7.65 at 95%, 8.22 at 98%, 9.62 at 100% ([review](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1316858.pdf)). Video: documentary comprehension rose from 62% at 87% coverage to 92% at 99% (Durbahn et al. 2020, same review).

**How much natives know.** Brysbaert et al. (2016), n = 221,268: the average 20-year-old American English speaker knows ~42,000 lemmas (5th–95th percentile 27,000–52,000), rising to ~48,200 at 60 (≈1 new lemma every 2 days); expressed in word families, ~11,100 of 18,269 families tested at 20 and ~13,400 at 60 ([Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01116/full)) **[strong]**. The older Goulden, Nation & Read (1990) estimate was ~17,200 word families (as cited by Brysbaert). So a 3,000-family core is ~15–25% of a native family vocabulary, not 10% — but it is *far* less than 10% of the lemma/word-form space.

**Core lists for survival speaking.**
- **CEFR sizes** (Milton 2010, X-Lex yes/no test over the most frequent 5,000 lemmatised words): A1 < 1,500; A2 1,500–2,500; B1 2,500–3,250; B2 3,250–3,750; C1 3,750–4,500; C2 4,500–5,000 ([Milton, EUROSLA monograph PDF](https://eurosla.org/monographs/EM02/Milton.pdf)) **[strong for English; the 5,000 ceiling is a test artefact]**. Vocabulary size explains up to 72% of variance in reading, ~52% writing, ~39% listening, 40–61% speaking (Stæhr; Milton et al., same source); "the threshold of 2,000 word families appears particularly important for basic communicative competence."
- **English Vocabulary Profile** (Cambridge): 6,970 headwords across A1–C2, ~4,700 at A1–B2, assigned from learner-corpus evidence of what learners actually *use* ([Capel 2012](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/418955FC7ED2455E98A499BC40C2C816/S2041536212000013a.pdf/completing-the-english-vocabulary-profile-c1-and-c2-vocabulary.pdf)) **[strong as inventory]**. Blog estimates of ~500 families at A1 and ~1,000–1,500 at A2 ([Linglify](https://linglify.com/en/learn/en/blog/english-vocabulary-cefr-levels)) are **[anecdotal — unsourced]**.
- **Oxford 3000 / 5000**: frequency-and-relevance lists for A1–B2 and up to C1 respectively ([OUP](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/about/wordlists/oxford3000-5000)) **[product list; page 403 this session]**.
- **Fluent Forever "625 words"**: the 2,000-word General Service List minus abstract and English-centric items, topped up from a frequency dictionary to 625 concrete, picturable words. Even advocates note it lacks function words and grammar — "a solid basic foundation," not conversational competence ([summary](https://languagelearningdiy.com/625-words-to-learn-a-language/)) **[anecdotal]**. Ogden's Basic English (850 words) is the ancestor of the idea.

### Critical caveats

1. **Function words dominate the top 100.** The 50% covered by the top-100 forms is mostly *the, of, and, to, a, in…* — near-zero meaning. The residual 5% of tokens at 95% coverage carries a disproportionate share of the content, which is why comprehension is only ~50–60% at 90–95% coverage (Schmitt et al.) **[strong]**.
2. **Text-type dependence.** The same 2,000 families cover 89% of conversation but ~83% of newspapers; specialist domains shift the curve further ([Nation 2006](https://www.scienceguide.nl/wp-content/uploads/2017/11/nation-2006-vocabulary.pdf)).
3. **Counting unit matters 2–4×.** "3,000 word families" ≈ 6,000–10,000 word forms; Brysbaert's 42,000 lemmas vs Goulden's 17,000 families is largely a units difference.
4. **Recognition ≠ use.** Coverage studies measure receptive knowledge; productive fluency needs collocations, chunks and morphology, and vocabulary size explains only ~40–60% of speaking variance (Milton).
5. **Coverage is a floor.** Cobb (2007) showed words beyond the first 2,000 recur too rarely in ordinary reading to be acquired incidentally — the mid-frequency band must be taught deliberately ([Language Teaching replication proposal](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/how-much-vocabulary-is-needed-to-use-english-replication-of-van-zeeland-schmitt-2012-nation-2006-and-cobb-2007/1D217A56A2E0056E67802A6A8360FDDE)) **[strong]**.

### Defensible statement
"10% → 90%" is **true of tokens in everyday speech** (the 2,000 most frequent word families ≈ 89% of conversation; ~3,000 ≈ 95–96%) and **false of comprehension** (90% coverage ≈ half-understanding) and **false of production**. The honest version: *a core of ~2,000–3,000 word families plus high-frequency chunks lets you follow most everyday conversation and is the highest-leverage investment a learner can make; beyond that, the return per word drops by Zipf's law, and reading needs ~8,000–9,000 families.*

## 2. Spaced repetition, retrieval practice and session length

### The core effects

- **Distributed practice** — Cepeda et al. (2006): 839 assessments from 317 experiments in 184 articles; spacing beats massing, and the optimal inter-study interval (ISI) grows with the retention interval (RI) ([abstract page](https://www.yorku.ca/ncepeda/publications/CPVWR2006.html); [PDF](https://augmentingcognition.com/assets/Cepeda2006.pdf)) **[strong]**.
- **Optimal gap** — Cepeda et al. (2008), n = 1,354, 26 gap×RI combinations: optimal gaps were 1 day for a 7-day RI, 11 days for 35 days, 21 days for 70 and 350 days; the optimal gap ≈ 20% of RI at a few weeks, falling to ~5–10% at a year. Recall at the optimal gap was 64% higher than massed (d ≈ 1.1); recognition 26% higher ([ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED505660.pdf)) **[strong]**.
- **Forgetting curve replication** — Murre & Dros (2015) reproduced Ebbinghaus: savings 53% at 20 min, 51% at 1 h, 34% at 9 h, 29% at 1 day, 24% at 2 days, 21% at 6 days, 4% at 31 days; best fit is a power function, with a small upward "jump" at ~24 h plausibly due to sleep consolidation ([PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0120644)) **[mixed — n=1, faithful replication]**.
- **Testing effect** — Roediger & Karpicke (2006): restudy wins at 5 minutes; at 2 days and 1 week "prior testing produced substantially greater retention than studying," although learners felt more confident after restudy ([Psychological Science](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x)) **[strong]**. Karpicke & Roediger (2008), 40 Swahili–English pairs: conditions that kept *testing* recalled ~80% after a week; conditions that dropped testing but kept studying recalled 33–36% (d = 4.03); learners predicted ~50% in every condition ([Science PDF](https://web.mit.edu/jbelcher/www/learner/retrieval.pdf)) **[strong]**. Meta-analytically, spaced vs massed retrieval is g = 0.74 ([Latimier, Peyre & Ramus 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-020-09572-8)) and spacing in L2 learning g ≈ 1.15 on delayed tests over 48 experiments (Kim & Webb 2022, [secondary summary](https://memormore.app/blogs/spaced-repetition-research)) **[strong; latter unverified this session]**.

### Expanding vs uniform intervals — genuinely mixed
- Karpicke & Roediger (2007): expanding (1-5-9) beat equal (5-5-5) at 10 min (71% vs 62%; 90% vs 87%) but **lost at 2 days** (33% vs 45%; 49% vs 60%; 44% vs 52%). The active ingredient was a *delayed, effortful first retrieval*, not the expansion ([PDF](https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2007/2007_Karpicke_Roediger_JEPLMC.pdf)) **[strong]**.
- Kang, Lindsey, Mozer & Pashler (2014), 60 Japanese–English pairs over 28 days, test at 56 days: expanding ≈ equal on the final test (.49 vs .46, n.s.), but expanding kept recallability higher *throughout* training and lost less between end of training and test ([Psychonomic Bulletin & Review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-014-0636-z)) **[strong]**.
- Meta-analysis: expanding vs uniform g = 0.034 (n.s.); expanding gains an edge only as the number of tests grows ([Latimier et al. 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-020-09572-8)) **[strong]**.
- **Design implication:** the schedule shape matters far less than (a) retrieval rather than re-reading, (b) a first review delayed enough to be effortful, and (c) enough total spaced reviews. Adaptive schedulers (below) win because they *personalise* the delay, not because they expand.

### Set size, interleaving and interference
- Nakata & Webb (2016): whether 20 words are learned as one set or as sets of 4/10 "has little effect"; spacing dominates set size ([SSLA](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/abs/does-studying-vocabulary-in-smaller-sets-increase-learning/E17B75ABAE1300734AF014C363D59FBC)) **[strong]**. So 3-item micro-sessions are not inherently worse than 20-item sessions *if the spacing is right*.
- Nakata & Suzuki (2019), 133 learners, 48 pairs: semantically related sets (mammals, organs) produced ~3× more within-set confusion errors than unrelated sets (8.22% vs 2.69% under massing), and spacing did **not** remove the interference; spacing helped unrelated words more ([SSLA](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/effects-of-massing-and-spacing-on-the-learning-of-semantically-related-and-unrelated-words/F58BA8D70385603B9C42E408BFCB8A10)) **[strong]**. Do not introduce "the days of the week" or "colours" as one batch.

### Algorithms
- **Leitner / Pimsleur / SM-2.** SM-2 (SuperMemo 1987, Anki's default until 2023): one state variable, an ease factor starting at 2.5 (±0.15/0.20 per rating, floor 1.3); new cards go 1 day → 6 days → interval × ease ([DeepWiki summary of fsrs-optimizer docs](https://deepwiki.com/open-spaced-repetition/fsrs-optimizer/7.3-comparison-with-sm-2)) **[strong as description]**.
- **Duolingo Half-Life Regression** (Settles & Meeder 2016): p = 2^(−Δ/h), h learned from counts of exposures/correct/incorrect plus per-word difficulty features, trained on 12.9 M learning traces. Offline MAE 0.128 vs Leitner 0.235 and Pimsleur 0.445 (AUC was essentially flat, 0.538 vs 0.542). Online: the deployed variant improved daily retention (any activity) by +12.0%, lessons +1.7%, practice +9.5% vs the earlier HLR ([ACL PDF](https://research.duolingo.com/papers/settles.acl16.pdf); [code](https://github.com/duolingo/halflife-regression)) **[strong]**. Its successor "Birdbrain" (2020) is an exercise-difficulty/knowledge-tracing model rather than a public scheduler **[unverified this session]**.
- **FSRS** (Ye et al.). Three-component model: Difficulty (1–10), Stability (the interval at which R = 90%) and Retrievability R(t,S) = (1 + FACTOR·t/S)^(−w20) (trainable decay in FSRS-6); next interval I(r,S) = S/FACTOR · (r^(1/DECAY) − 1) for a chosen desired retention r; same-day reviews grow stability by a damped exponential, S·e^(w17(G−3+w18))·S^(−w19) ([algorithm wiki](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/awesome-fsrs/wiki/The-Algorithm)) **[strong as description]**. Built into Anki since 23.10 ([fsrs4anki](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/fsrs4anki)).
- **Benchmark** (open-spaced-repetition/srs-benchmark, ~350 M reviews from ~10,000 Anki users, time-series split), log loss / RMSE(bins) / AUC: FSRS-7 (recency) 0.3414 / 0.0627 / 0.710; FSRS-6 0.3460 / 0.0653 / 0.703; HLR 0.4694 / 0.1275 / 0.637; "always predict the user's average" 0.3945 / 0.1034 / 0.500 ([repo](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/srs-benchmark); [Expertium's summary](https://expertium.github.io/Benchmark.html)) **[strong — but it measures recall *prediction*, not learning outcomes]**. FSRS-6 beats Anki-SM-2 in 99.6% of collections; the earlier ankitects benchmark (71 users, 4.6 M reviews) had FSRS v4 at log loss 0.387 vs SM-2 0.732 and RMSE(bins) 0.046 vs 0.208 ([ankitects/fsrs-benchmark](https://github.com/ankitects/fsrs-benchmark)). The maintainers call FSRS-7 "the final version." Note that HLR — the one model trained on *language-app* data — scores below the constant-average baseline here; its 3 parameters underfit.

### Session length and micro-sessions — what the evidence supports
- **Many tiny sessions vs one long one: no direct RCT found.** What exists is (a) the spacing literature, which says *between-session gaps of hours-to-days* are what matter (Cepeda 2006/2008; Nakata & Webb 2016), and (b) the FSRS same-day formula, reflecting the empirical fact that repeated same-day reviews add little long-term stability compared with a review after sleep (Murre & Dros's 24-h bump). Inference **[mixed]**: splitting a day's reviews into several micro-sessions is roughly neutral for retention *per review* and positive for adherence; re-reviewing the *same* item several times in one day is mostly wasted.
- **WaitChatter** (Cai et al., CHI 2015): 20 users, 2 weeks, French/Spanish flashcards inside a chat client while waiting for replies. Median exercise 1.83 s; ~4 new words/day (57 over two weeks; range 17–256); 66% L2 recall and 92% L1 recognition on a post-test; response rate highest right after the user *sent* a message (49.1%) and lowest when the chat window was unfocused (31.2%); enjoyment 6.15/7 ([CHI PDF](https://sls.csail.mit.edu/publications/2015/Cai_CHI-2015.pdf); [MIT news](https://www.csail.mit.edu/node/1321)) **[mixed — small n]**. WaitSuite (TOCHI 2017) generalised to Wi-Fi waits, elevators and pull-to-refresh: microtasks suit waits "where the switch time … is less than the waiting duration, and when cognitive demand is low"; ~50-second elevator waits worked, sub-second Wi-Fi connects did not ([publication list](https://people.csail.mit.edu/ccai/publications.html); [New Atlas](https://newatlas.com/mit-csail-wait-learning-language-apps/49038/)) **[mixed]**.
- **MicroMandarin** (Edge et al., CHI 2011): 23 users, 4 weeks; the location-contextual version yielded sessions half as long (10.7 vs 23.6 cards) in twice as many places (9.7 vs 5.1), but the frequency-ordered version taught more cards (41.3 vs 19.1) — partly by in-session cramming ([Microsoft Research PDF](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/edge-CHI2011-micromandarin.pdf)) **[mixed]**.
- **Dingler et al. (MobileHCI 2017), "Language learning on-the-go"** — survey plus in-the-wild study of opportune moments for phone-based vocabulary micro-sessions ([ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3098279.3098565)) **[unverified this session — full text inaccessible]**.
- **How much "dead time" exists:** experience sampling of 21 U.S. workers found ~50 minutes of waiting per day (mean episode 18 min, median 11.5), 57% spent on leisure and 22.5% on "productive" activity ([arXiv 2404.02880](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.02880v1)) **[mixed]**. Micro-learning competes with scrolling, not with work.
- **Minimum effective dose:** a double-blind RCT reported ~3 min/day of app-based spaced repetition roughly tripling vocabulary retention (Chukharev-Hudilainen & Klepikova 2016, CALICO, [secondary summary](https://memormore.app/blogs/spaced-repetition-research)) **[unverified this session]**. Drops' 5-minute daily cap is product folklore, not evidence **[anecdotal]**.

## 3. Notifications as the delivery channel

- **Duolingo's bandit for reminder copy** (Yancey & Settles, KDD 2020): Duolingo sends **one** practice reminder per day; the "sleeping, recovering bandit" chooses among dozens of message templates, modelling that a template's effectiveness *decays* when reused recently and *recovers* with rest, and reported significant lifts in daily active users in production ([ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3394486.3403351)) **[unverified this session — paywalled; the one-per-day cadence and decay/recovery design are the load-bearing facts]**. Lesson: copy novelty is a depletable resource — rotate.
- **Timing beats volume.** WaitChatter is the cleanest evidence that *when* a prompt lands matters more than *how many*: 49% response at a natural breakpoint vs 31% when the user is elsewhere ([Cai 2015](https://sls.csail.mit.edu/publications/2015/Cai_CHI-2015.pdf)) **[mixed]**. Pielot et al. (UbiComp 2015) inferred boredom from phone-usage signals and found users more willing to engage with pushed content when predicted bored ([ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2750858.2804252)) **[unverified this session]**. All such signals (unlock, app switch, idle time, charging) are available **on-device** without telemetry.
- **Fatigue and uninstalls.** The most-cited industry figure is a 2015 Localytics survey: 46% said they would disable push at 2–5 notifications per week and 32% would abandon the app at 6–10 per week **[anecdotal — self-report; URL not retrievable this session]**. No peer-reviewed study of learning-notification opt-out rates was found. Lock-screen/widget "passive learning" claims ("96 unlocks a day") are marketing without cited data ([example](https://wordify.it/blog/lock-screen-widgets-that-teach)) **[anecdotal]**.
- **Interactive (answer-in-notification) quizzes:** no controlled study located; WaitChatter's 2-second inline exercise is the nearest analogue and its result — high enjoyment, ~4 words/day — is encouraging **[mixed]**.
- **Tolerable cadence (inference):** one unsolicited push per day is the evidence-backed baseline (Duolingo); allow more only when (a) the user has opted into "sprinkle mode," (b) an on-device signal suggests a natural breakpoint, and (c) an item is actually due. Everything else should be *pull* (widget, lock-screen glance, in-app), not *push*.

## 4. "Subconscious embedding": what is real and what is myth

- **Attention is required.** Schmidt (1990): "subliminal language learning is impossible"; noticing is "the necessary and sufficient condition for converting input to intake" ([Applied Linguistics](https://academic.oup.com/applij/article-abstract/11/2/129/163482)) **[strong as consensus; the "sufficient" half is contested]**.
- **Sleep learning:** you cannot learn new words asleep. Schreiner & Rasch (2015): 120 Dutch–German pairs learned *awake*; replaying the Dutch cues during NREM sleep improved later recall of those pairs vs uncued pairs; the same cueing during wakefulness did nothing ([Cerebral Cortex](https://academic.oup.com/cercor/article-abstract/25/11/4169/2366428)) **[strong for reactivation; irrelevant for acquisition]**. Design corollary: what helps is *good encoding before sleep*, i.e., an evening review session; not audio played overnight.
- **Implicit grammar learning exists but is modest and still tied to awareness.** Rebuschat & Williams (2012): incidental exposure to an artificial syntax produced some unconscious knowledge, but conscious (unverbalisable) knowledge correlated with better grammaticality judgements ([Applied Psycholinguistics](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/applied-psycholinguistics/article/abs/implicit-and-explicit-knowledge-in-second-language-acquisition/D73232D38AFF07F94E7B0BE599CF88B5)) **[mixed]**.
- **Incidental vocabulary acquisition is real but slow and dose-dependent.** Webb (2007), 121 learners: 1/3/7/10 encounters in context; "if learners encounter unknown words ten times in context, sizeable learning gains may occur," but full knowledge needs more than ten ([Applied Linguistics](https://academic.oup.com/applij/article-abstract/28/1/46/174744)) **[strong]**. The Uchihara, Webb & Yanagisawa (2019) meta-analysis puts the frequency–learning correlation at roughly r ≈ .3 with wide moderation ([Language Learning](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/lang.12343)) **[unverified this session]**. Watching a full-length TV programme yields measurable meaning-recognition and recall gains, moderated by frequency of occurrence, prior vocabulary and cognateness (Peters & Webb 2018, [SSLA](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/abs/incidental-vocabulary-acquisition-through-viewing-l2-television-and-factors-that-affect-learning/0E45A630F37C48A5BDB6CC3F725ADDC9)) **[strong]**. Mid-frequency words simply do not recur often enough in natural input for this to be efficient (Cobb 2007) **[strong]**.
- **Passive/ambient exposure without attention** (background audio, unread lock-screen text) has no supporting evidence for vocabulary and is contradicted by the noticing literature **[strong negative]**. Karpicke & Roediger's data show even *attended* re-reading adds ~nothing once an item has been recalled once — passive glancing is weaker still.

**Defensible reframing.** What *is* real: (1) retrieval that is effortful but brief, (2) spaced across days, (3) sleep-consolidated, (4) delivered at moments of low cognitive load so it never registers as "study," and (5) eventually *automatised* so that recall feels effortless — the subjective experience users call "it just came to me." The honest promise: **"low-friction, high-frequency retrieval that doesn't feel like studying — and that, after enough spaced recalls, becomes automatic."** Not "learn without noticing."

## 5. Attrition and maintenance ("keeping languages alive")

- **Bahrick (1984)**, 773 people tested up to 50 years after school Spanish: most loss occurs in the first ~3–6 years; what survives that window stays essentially flat for 25+ years ("permastore"); the amount retained scales with original training depth and grades, and rehearsal during the interval mattered little ([JEP: General, DOI 10.1037/0096-3445.113.1.1](https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-3445.113.1.1)) **[strong; unverified this session — APA blocks robots]**.
- **Bahrick & Phelps (1987)**: 50 Spanish–English pairs learned with 0-, 1- or 30-day gaps between sessions; after **8 years** the 30-day group retained roughly double the 1-day group (≈15% vs ≈8%) ([JEP:LMC, DOI 10.1037/0278-7393.13.2.344](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.13.2.344); [secondary summary](https://memormore.app/blogs/spaced-repetition-research)) **[strong; figures via secondary source]**.
- **Bahrick, Bahrick, Bahrick & Bahrick (1993)**: 300 foreign-word pairs, 13 or 26 relearning sessions at 14-, 28- or 56-day intervals, tested at 1, 2, 3 and 5 years. "Thirteen retraining sessions spaced at 56 days yielded retention comparable to 26 sessions spaced at 14 days"; spacing and number of sessions had independent benefits regardless of word difficulty ([Psychological Science](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-9280.1993.tb00571.x)) **[strong — n=4 but multi-year within-subject]**. This is the closest thing to a quantified *maintenance dose*: **one successful retrieval every ~2 months holds a known word for years**.
- **Savings on relearning** are large: even at 31 days with ~4% raw savings in Murre & Dros's replication, relearning to criterion took a fraction of original learning ([PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0120644)) **[mixed]**; Bahrick's cohorts relearned "lost" Spanish far faster than novices **[unverified this session]**.
- **L2 attrition research** (Schmid & Mehotcheva 2012) distinguishes attrition of *instructed* foreign languages from immersion-acquired L2, and the literature consistently reports that productive skills and low-frequency/late-learned vocabulary go first, receptive knowledge and high-frequency items last, with initial proficiency the strongest protector ([DuJAL](https://www.jbe-platform.com/content/journals/10.1075/dujal.1.1.08sch)) **[mixed; the abstract confirms the framing, the skill-ordering claims are literature consensus, unverified this session]**.

**Maintenance-mode implications:** (1) the danger zone is the first months–years after active study stops; (2) the required dose is small but must be *retrieval*, not re-exposure; (3) target intervals should lengthen toward 1–3 months per item; (4) productive recall (L1→L2) needs protecting more than recognition; (5) relearning is cheap, so a lapse is recoverable and the UI should say so.

## 6. Multi-language SRS: interleaving and on-device FSRS

**Interleaving across languages — no direct study found.** The applicable evidence is interference research: semantically related items confused in the same session (Nakata & Suzuki 2019, above) and the general finding that spacing beats blocking *across days* but that mixing confusable material *within* a session raises error rates. Practical rule **[inference]**: keep each micro-session (3–5 items) **within one language** (blocked), but **interleave languages across sessions** during the day; keep separate FSRS parameter sets per language (difficulty distributions differ); tag every item by language and avoid scheduling cross-language false friends/cognates (Spanish *embarazada* / Portuguese *embaraçada*) into adjacent sessions. Anki practice (separate decks per language, sometimes a daily "one language per session" habit) is consistent but anecdotal ([Anki forum example](https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/fsrs-vs-sm2-in-2-seperate-profiles/52171)) **[anecdotal]**.

## 7. Synthesis

**(a) The 90-10 rule, stated defensibly.** Word frequencies follow Zipf's law, so a small core does most of the work: the ~2,000 most frequent word families cover about 89% of the words in everyday conversation and ~3,000 cover 95–96% (Nation 2006; van Zeeland & Schmitt 2013). That core is the best investment a learner can make, and it is enough to *follow* most everyday speech. But coverage is not comprehension: at 90–95% coverage learners understand only about half to two-thirds of a text, comprehension rises roughly linearly to 100% (Schmitt et al. 2011), the missing words carry most of the meaning, and speaking needs chunks, grammar and productive recall that a word count does not capture. So: "a 3,000-word core gets you 95% of the *words* and a working foundation — not 90% of the *language*."

**(b) All-day micro-SRS parameters** (derived from §2–3):
- **Items per micro-session:** 3–5 retrieval items, ~30–90 s (WaitChatter: ~2 s per exercise plus feedback; Nakata & Webb: spacing, not set size, is the lever).
- **Sessions per day:** 3–6 opportunities, only the *first* push-initiated by default; the rest are widget/lock-screen/in-app pulls or opt-in "sprinkle" prompts. Push ceiling: **1/day default, hard cap 3/day with explicit opt-in**, never within 60 min of the last interaction, never in quiet hours.
- **Scheduling:** FSRS-6, desired retention 0.85–0.90; *no item repeats within a day* unless it failed; first retrieval of a new item delayed to the next session (Karpicke & Roediger 2007).
- **Content mix:** ≥60% retrieval (L1→L2 or gap-fill), ≤2 brand-new items per session, ≤10–15 new items/day to bound the backlog; never a batch of semantically related items (Nakata & Suzuki 2019).
- **Timing signals (on-device):** unlock after ≥20 min idle, app-switch to a "waiting" context, charging, habitual time-of-day; a small local bandit over time slots and copy variants with decay/recovery, à la Duolingo, needs no server.
- **Stop for the day** when due items = 0 or after ~10 minutes cumulative; say "done" rather than manufacturing reviews. Prefer an evening session (sleep consolidation).

**(c) What to promise about "subconscious."** Say: "Tiny, frequent recall — a few seconds at a time — that doesn't feel like studying. Do it long enough and the words stop needing effort." Do not say "learn while you're not paying attention," "learn in your sleep," or "passively absorb"; the noticing literature and the sleep-cueing data forbid it. The honest claim is *automaticity through spaced retrieval*.

**(d) Maintenance-mode spec.** Trigger when a user pauses a language or most items' FSRS stability exceeds ~60 days. Then: (1) one *productive* retrieval per known item every 8–12 weeks (Bahrick 1993's 56-day result; FSRS at desired retention ~0.80 approximates it) — for a 2,000-word core that is ~25–35 items/day, ~2–3 minutes; (2) prioritise the first 12–24 months after active study, the steepest attrition window (Bahrick 1984); (3) weight L1→L2 recall over recognition; (4) let cadence fall to a weekly 10-minute "keep-alive" without failing the user, and frame lapses as cheap to recover (relearning savings); (5) keep it per-language, so pausing Portuguese never floods the Spanish queue.
