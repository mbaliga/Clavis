# Learning several languages at once: what the research supports, what it warns against, and how to design around it

Research memo prepared 2026-08-29 for a product designer in Hyderabad (native Hindi + Konkani; professional English; elementary Spanish and Japanese; limited Urdu) planning a multi-language app that (a) teaches several target languages simultaneously, (b) groups targets by language family / shared vocabulary strata, and (c) replaces rote memorisation with etymology, loanword and cognate awareness.

**How to read the evidence labels.** `STRONG` = replicated experimental/quantitative findings or meta-analyses. `MIXED` = real studies but small, conflicting, or not directly on the design question. `PRACTITIONER` = polyglot/teacher advice without controlled evidence. `NOT VERIFIED` = a claim I could not confirm from a primary source this session (my web-search budget was exhausted part-way; everything else below was read from the cited page).

---

## 1. Learning multiple foreign languages at the same time

### 1.1 Third-language acquisition (TLA) theory: what the models actually predict

**Cenoz — the "additive effect" of bilingualism on L3.** Cenoz's review concludes that prior bilingualism generally helps L3 acquisition, and attributes it to metalinguistic awareness, communicative skill and "the experience acquired ... in the process of second language acquisition" (Cenoz 2003, *International Journal of Bilingualism*, https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/13670069030070010501). The advantage is conditional: it appears in *additive* contexts and, per Cummins' threshold hypothesis cited there, presupposes reasonable literacy/proficiency in the earlier languages. `STRONG for "prior languages help"; MIXED on size and conditions.`

**Jessner — Dynamic Model of Multilingualism (DMM) and the "M-factor".** Herdina & Jessner's DMM (2002) and Jessner's later work treat the multilingual system as dynamic, with an emergent "multilingualism factor" whose core is heightened metalinguistic awareness ("the ability to focus on linguistic form and to switch focus between form and meaning", Jessner 2008 as quoted in Jung 2013, https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1176964.pdf). The best recent quantitative test I could read: Dolas, Jessner & Cedden (2022, *Journal of Cognition*, https://journalofcognition.org/articles/10.5334/joc.201) tested 117 Turkish-L1 children aged 11–14 (mono-, bi- and multilingual, regular vs gifted programmes). Multilingualism correlated with metalinguistic awareness at r = .62 in the non-gifted group; multilingualism (β = .44, p = .002) and vocabulary (β = .41) predicted metalinguistic awareness in the gifted group; multilinguals outperformed mono- and bilinguals on all measures. `MIXED→STRONG (correlational, one context, but consistent with the wider literature).` Adults: early bilinguals (English–Spanish and English–Mandarin) outperformed monolinguals at learning phonologically unfamiliar novel words (Kaushanskaya & Marian 2009, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*, https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/PBR.16.4.705). `STRONG for a general word-learning advantage in bilinguals.`

**Where transfer comes from: three competing models (all matter for your app).**

- *Typological Primacy Model* (Rothman 2011, *Second Language Research*, https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0267658310386439). Two groups: L1 Italian/L2 English→L3 Spanish and L1 English/L2 Spanish→L3 Brazilian Portuguese, low-to-intermediate L3. Both transferred a subtle adjective-placement semantics from the *typologically closer* language (Italian and Spanish respectively), not from L1 or L2 per se. Rothman's 2015 elaboration (https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/43601/; the abstract page was blocked for me, so details are `NOT VERIFIED`) proposes that the parser assesses similarity early via a hierarchy of cues — lexicon first, then phonology, morphology, syntax — and that transfer is wholesale and happens early, which is why it can be *non-facilitative* where the "closest" language actually differs.
- *L2 Status Factor* (Bardel & Falk 2007, *Second Language Research*, https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0267658307080557). Learners of L3 Swedish/Dutch placed sentence negation according to their L2, not L1: "syntactic structures are more easily transferred from L2 than from L1 in the initial state of L3 acquisition." Their 2021 update stresses that L2 proficiency and age of acquisition modulate this (Bardel & Falk 2021, https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0267658320941033).
- *Psychotypology* (Kellerman 1977/1978/1983). Learners transfer on the basis of *perceived* distance: "if the learner believes that there could be a relationship between NL and TL at a given point, he may well transfer" (Kellerman 1977, quoted in Athmani & Boukhedimi 2021, *Arab World English Journal*, https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1323983.pdf). In that 2021 replication, 77.5% of 40 Arabic-L1/French-L2 Algerian students perceived French as closer to English and 65% used French to fill gaps when writing English. Kellerman's original Dutch *breken/break* transferability experiments (1983, "Now you see it, now you don't", in Gass & Selinker eds.) are `NOT VERIFIED` this session.

A useful review that lines these up, including proficiency and recency effects, is Wang (2011, Columbia *SALT* working papers, https://journals.library.columbia.edu/index.php/SALT/article/download/1342/405/3406): lower L3 proficiency → stronger L2 influence; Cenoz (2001) found Spanish accounted for ~72% of transferred items among Basque–Spanish bilinguals learning English.

**Design implication:** the source of transfer is *predictable* (perceived closeness + most-recent non-native language + lexicon-first). Your app can therefore anticipate which language will "leak" into which and pre-empt it.

### 1.2 Typological closeness is not uniformly helpful

Gil (2026, *Second Language Research*, https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/02676583251389463) reports two studies: 49 Japanese-L1 vs 26 English-L1 learners of Korean (wh-indefinites) — the *English* group did better despite Japanese being structurally near-identical; and 72 Chinese vs 83 Korean learners of English experiential constructions — the structurally *distant* Korean group did better. Her account: when languages are close, learners must *restructure* an entrenched near-match (hard); when they are distant they *develop* a new structure (comparatively easy). "Microvariation between typologically close languages can impede L2 learning." `MIXED (two studies, one author) but mechanistically important.`

### 1.3 Interference and blending in sibling languages

- **Spanish/Portuguese.** Lipski (2006, *Selected Proceedings of the 8th Hispanic Linguistics Symposium*, https://www.lingref.com/cpp/hls/8/paper1251.pdf) documents "portuñol" hybrid forms produced by Spanish speakers "faking" Portuguese — blended verb forms (*yo terê*), article–noun mismatches (*del ousados hombres*), Spanish diphthongisation applied to Portuguese — and argues that the very similarity "creates interference rather than facilitating learning". Carvalho & da Silva (2006, *Foreign Language Annals*, https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ741850): Spanish–English bilinguals learning Portuguese subjunctive, think-aloud protocols; *both* L1-English and L1-Spanish groups "rely heavily on Spanish" — "linguistic similarity between the languages overrides order of acquisition." `STRONG that Spanish dominates transfer into Portuguese whatever the L1.`
- **Lexical blends in Italian/Spanish/French.** De Angelis & Selinker (2001) analysed two adult multilinguals learning Italian: phonologically similar items from other non-native Romance languages "compete for selection" in production, driven by perceived closeness and a "foreign-language effect" (summarised in Wang 2011, above). Williams & Hammarberg (1998, *Applied Linguistics*, https://academic.oup.com/applij/article-abstract/19/3/295/162251) tracked 844 non-adapted switches over two years in an L1-English/L2-German learner of L3 Swedish: German (the closest, recent, non-native language) became the *default supplier* of lexical material, English stayed *instrumental* (metalinguistic comments). `STRONG (case-study evidence, widely replicated in the TLA literature).`
- **Comprehension is where similarity pays; production is where it bites.** Ringbom (2007, *Cross-linguistic Similarity in Foreign Language Learning*, https://www.multilingual-matters.com/page/detail/?k=9781853599347): in comprehension of related languages similarities "are easily perceived"; in production "frequently similarities are here merely assumed", and learners first set up "oversimplified" one-to-one equivalences that later have to be unlearned. `STRONG conceptual framework, backed by the Finnish/Swedish-Finn English data in his Ch. 6.`

### 1.4 Empirical studies of learners studying two new languages concurrently

Surprisingly few, none on two *sibling* languages at beginner level:

| Study | Languages | Design | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rahmatian & Farshadjou 2013, *International Education Studies* 6(10), https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1068706.pdf | Persian L1; English + French | 36 adults (20–29); 12 simultaneous (6 double-beginners, 6 intermediate-English/beginner-French) vs 24 single-language controls; 20 sessions; Cambridge placement + exams /80 | Simultaneous groups scored *higher* in French (67.3 vs 52.3; 69.3 vs 59.5). Authors: simultaneous learning "reinforces learning of the two languages". Tiny n; no delayed test. |
| Huang, Steinkrauss & Verspoor 2022, *International Journal of Multilingualism* 19(4):566–582, https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1370864 | Chinese L1; English only vs English + Russian | One academic year; L2 English writing gains and variability (Complex Dynamic Systems framing) | "The L2 + L3 learners did not develop their L2 to a lesser extent"; only more *variability* in writing fluency. |
| Libersky, Sobus, Slawny & Kaushanskaya 2025, *Second Language Research*, https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/02676583251338768 | English L1; German + Polish vocabulary | Lab: interleaved vs blocked presentation of the two languages; Exp. 2 added a mid-session break | Interleaving the two languages *beat* blocking (Exp. 1); a rest break removed the difference, so the benefit is probably a spacing effect. |
| Wu 2023, *LNEP* 18:120–124, https://lnep.ewapub.com/article/view/5380 | Review of the above two | Student conference paper | Not independent evidence. |

`MIXED but consistently non-negative: no study shows harm from concurrent study of two typologically distinct languages; two show benefit.` What is missing is exactly your case: two closely related targets, both at A0–A2, with productive goals.

### 1.5 Similar vs dissimilar pairs, and what practitioners say

- **Dissimilar pairs concurrently:** supported (the three studies above all used distant pairs; Kaushanskaya's interleaving benefit was with German + Polish). `MIXED→STRONG.`
- **Similar pairs concurrently at beginner level, productive goals:** no supporting study; the sibling-language interference literature (Lipski; Carvalho & da Silva; Williams & Hammarberg; Ringbom; Gil) gives principled reasons to expect blends and slowed restructuring. `Risky; not evidence-based.`
- **Similar pairs concurrently for receptive skills:** strongly supported by the intercomprehension programmes (Section 2).
- **Practitioner advice** (`PRACTITIONER`): the two practitioner pieces I could read both recommend (i) get "a basic grasp of the top-priority language" before adding another unless you already are a polyglot, (ii) "choose two very different languages" to reduce interference, (iii) weight time unevenly (e.g. 80% to the harder one) and keep separate sessions/contexts (LiveLingua, https://www.livelingua.com/blog/should-i-study-one-language-at-a-time-or-many/; Talkio, https://www.talkio.ai/blog/what-happens-if-you-study-two-languages-at-once-surprising-science-behind-double-language-learning). The common polyglot heuristics "one language at a time until ~B1" and "laddering" (learning L3 *through* L2, e.g. Portuguese via Spanish materials) are widespread in polyglot writing (Lampariello, Kaufmann, Wyner) — `NOT VERIFIED` from primary pages this session and in any case anecdotal. Note that laddering has partial theoretical backing: it deliberately exploits TPM/L2-status transfer and keeps the "default supplier" language as the medium rather than a rival target.

## 2. Language-family leverage and intercomprehension

### 2.1 What the programmes are and what they claim

**EuroCom / EuroComRom "seven sieves"** (Klein & Stegmann 1999/2000; English edition McCann, Klein & Stegmann 2003, https://books.google.com/books/about/EuroComRom_the_Seven_Sieves.html?id=2HjRAAAACAAJ). The sieves are transfer techniques for *reading* an unknown sibling language: (1) international vocabulary, (2) pan-Romance vocabulary, (3) sound correspondences, (4) spelling/pronunciation, (5) pan-Romance syntactic patterns, (6) morphosyntactic elements, (7) "Eurofixes" (shared prefixes/suffixes) plus a short list of language-specific "profile words" (Grzega 2005, *Journal for EuroLinguistiX*, https://www1.ku.de/SLF/EngluVglSW/ELiX/grzega-051.pdf). Sister programmes exist for Germanic (EuroComGerm) and Slavic (EuroComSlav).

**Romance intercomprehension** (Galatea, EuRom4/EuRom5, Galanet 2003, Galapro 2009, Redinter 2009–11, Miriadi 2015–, InterRom in Córdoba). All are explicitly *receptive*: "understanding (reading, listening) rather than producing" (Wikipedia overview, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intercomprehension; Politeja 2019, https://journals.akademicka.pl/politeja/article/view/1225). The EuRom5 authors state that learners "understand texts dealing with general topics in a short time (approximately 35/40 h)" across Portuguese, Spanish, Catalan, Italian and French (Bonvino, Fiorenza & Cortés Velásquez 2018, *Frontiers in Communication*, https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2018.00029/full). **Caveat:** that 35–40 h figure is a programme claim; the same paper presents strategy analysis, not pre/post scores, and a São Paulo replication with Italian learners (Baraldi & Santoro 2020, https://revistas.usp.br/italianistica/en/article/view/174438) reports only perception questionnaires. `PRACTITIONER/programme evidence; the quantitative outcome base is thin.`

### 2.2 The hard numbers: how much of a sibling language is understood with no training

The best dataset is the MICReLa project (Gooskens, van Heuven, Golubović, Schüppert, Swarte & Voigt 2017, *International Journal of Multilingualism*, https://www.let.rug.nl/gooskens/pdf/publ_ijm_2017.pdf): 1,833 university-educated listeners aged 18–33, 16 languages, spoken cloze test (r = .97 with self-estimated intelligibility). Selected scores (listener → test language, all participants):

- Portuguese listeners hearing Spanish **77.4%**; Spanish listeners hearing Portuguese **37.2%**
- Italian listeners hearing Spanish **65.7%**; Spanish listeners hearing Italian **45.7%**
- Italian listeners hearing Portuguese **49.4%**; Portuguese listeners hearing Italian **33.5%**
- Spanish listeners hearing French **28.2%**; French listeners hearing Spanish **31.5%**
- Czech↔Slovak **92.7 / 95.0%**; Slovene listeners hearing Croatian **79.4%** vs Croatian→Slovene **43.7%**
- Danish/Swedish ≈ **57–63%** (roughly symmetric once exposure is controlled)

The authors suggest ~40% cloze as a working threshold for viable receptive communication and explain the Spanish/Portuguese asymmetry structurally ("Portuguese has a rather complicated vowel system with nasalised vowels and a high prevalence of assimilations, while the Spanish vowel system only has five vowels"). Earlier: Jensen (1989, *Hispania*, https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ400860) measured 50–60% listening comprehension between Spanish- and Portuguese-speaking students, Portuguese speakers doing better. Scandinavian history: Haugen's 1950s self-reports (56% of Danes claimed to understand spoken Swedish; 54% of Swedes Danish), Maurud's 1976 objective task (Swedes 23% on Danish, Danes 43% on Swedish), and Delsing & Lundin Åkesson (2005) confirming the asymmetry is "much less prevalent in written language" (all summarised in "Inter-Nordic Communication", *LEA*, https://oajournals.fupress.net/index.php/bsfm-lea/article/download/17206/15785/48601). `STRONG.`

**Interslavic** (a constructed pan-Slavic language) is the extreme case of family leverage: an online cloze survey with ~1,700 valid responses across all Slavic L1s averaged **84%** (range 79–93% by language), and 70 Bulgarian respondents translated 85–93% of test items with no prior training (Kocór, Yordanova, van Steenbergen & Merunka 2017, https://www.academia.edu/69195787/Zonal_Constructed_Language_and_Education_Support_of_e_Democracy_The_Interslavic_Experience). `MIXED (self-selected online sample) but striking.`

### 2.3 Testing your key hypothesis: "family approaches work for receptive skills and vocabulary; production in siblings should be staggered"

Evidence **for**:
1. Every mature intercomprehension programme restricts itself to reception by design (Section 2.1); none claims productive outcomes.
2. Marx (2012, *International Journal of Bilingualism*, https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1367006911429517): 73 German students read texts in five *unknown* Germanic languages; Dutch was easiest, and "lexical comprehension substantially outperformed structural comprehension" — vocabulary transfers first, grammar lags.
3. Ringbom's comprehension/production asymmetry (Section 1.3) and the production-side blend evidence (Lipski; De Angelis & Selinker; Williams & Hammarberg).
4. Gooskens, Kürschner & van Heuven (2022, *Nordic Journal of Linguistics*, https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/nordic-journal-of-linguistics/article/role-of-loanwords-in-the-intelligibility-of-written-danish-among-swedes/D1CE5C3E4DBF20D13773A35885A971CA): 42 Swedish teenagers translated 384 written Danish words; **loanwords 84.8% vs inherited cognates 73.3%** correct; Romance/Greek/English loans >90% vs Low/High German loans 68–72%; texts dense in loanwords were understood better. Receptive vocabulary transfer is real and quantifiable.
5. Gil (2026): fine-grained *productive* grammar in a close language requires restructuring, which is slow.

Evidence **against / qualifying**: no controlled study shows that *concurrent* productive study of two siblings is worse than sequential; the claim rests on mechanism and case studies, not RCTs. Also note the asymmetries: a "family" strategy for a Spanish learner is far cheaper for reading Portuguese than for *listening* to it (37% vs 77% in the reverse direction), so "receptive" must be split into reading vs listening.

**Verdict:** your hypothesis is well supported for reading and vocabulary, moderately for listening (language-pair-specific), and the staggering of production is a prudent inference rather than a demonstrated finding.

## 3. Cognate facilitation

**Cognates are learned faster and retained better.** `STRONG.`
- Lotto & de Groot (1998, *Language Learning*, https://www.academia.edu/12744827/Effects_of_Learning_Method_and_Word_Type_on_Acquiring_Vocabulary_in_an_Unfamiliar_Language): 56 Dutch adults learning Italian; recall **86.5% for cognates vs 72.0% for non-cognates**; high-frequency 82.6% vs low-frequency 75.9%; learning via L1 translation beat learning via pictures, and the cognate advantage persisted even with picture cues.
- de Groot & Keijzer (2000, *Language Learning*, https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ601532): paired-associate training; cognates and concrete words "were easier to learn and less susceptible to forgetting than noncognates" — "what is hard to learn is easy to forget."
- de Groot (2006, *Language Learning*, https://annettedegroot.com/content/4-publications/de-groot-ll-2006-effects-of-stimulus-characteristics-and-background-music-on-foreign-language-vocabulary-learning-and-forgetting.pdf): one-week retention — concrete words fell 89.4%→62.7%, abstract 81.8%→41.7%; typical-form words 89.5%→58.5% vs atypical 81.7%→45.8%. Easy-to-learn material is forgotten least.

**Learners do not exploit cognates automatically; instruction helps.** `STRONG (children), MIXED (adults).`
- Nagy, García, Durgunoğlu & Hancin-Bhatt (1993, *Journal of Reading Behavior*, https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/10862969009547816): 74 Spanish–English bilingual children (grades 4–6) recognised only about *half* of available cognates even when told to look; when they knew the Spanish cognate they understood the English word 67% of the time vs 37% otherwise; the Spanish-vocabulary→English-comprehension link was positive only for good cognate recognisers (r = .52 vs −.60); suffix-changing cognates (*reality/realidad*) were hardest.
- Dressler, Carlo, Snow, August & White (2011, *Bilingualism: Language and Cognition*, https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/bilingualism-language-and-cognition/article/abs/spanishspeaking-students-use-of-cognate-knowledge-to-infer-the-meaning-of-english-words/2F90115913F4DA596F4E4C8FF85E738A): cognate recognition was associated with explicit strategy instruction, metalinguistic skill, and the structural transparency of the pair.
- Otwinowska's Polish–English programme (book: *Cognate Vocabulary in Language Acquisition and Use*, Multilingual Matters 2015, https://www.multilingual-matters.com/page/detail/Cognate-Vocabulary-in-Language-Acquisition-and-Use/?k=9781783094370; classroom experiment: Otwinowska et al. 2020, *Language Learning*, https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/lang.12390) investigates awareness, strategy use and the age factor. The 2020 abstract was blocked for me, so its exact effect sizes are `NOT VERIFIED`.

**False friends: the risk is real but smaller and different than assumed.** `MIXED→STRONG.`
- Marecka, Szewczyk, Otwinowska, Durlik, Foryś-Nogala, Kutyłowska & Wodniecka (2021, *Cognition*, https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/en/works/96QQ8zNl/; DOI 10.1016/j.cognition.2020.104477): false cognates were learned *faster than non-cognates* in word-form learning — orthographic overlap helps you remember the *form* even when the meaning differs. The problem with false friends is therefore semantic (wrong meaning persists), not memorial. Companion paper: Otwinowska & Szewczyk 2019, *International Journal of Bilingual Education and Bilingualism*, https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13670050.2017.1325834 (blocked; `NOT VERIFIED`).
- Ringbom's "assumed similarity" (Section 1.3) is the mechanism: in production the learner presumes equivalence.

## 4. Etymology, loanwords and morphology as learning aids

**Depth of processing / elaboration.** Craik & Lockhart's levels-of-processing framework (1972, *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior*, DOI 10.1016/S0022-5371(72)80001-X — canonical; page blocked, `NOT VERIFIED` this session) predicts that semantically elaborated encodings are more durable. Direct L2 tests:
- **Etymological elaboration for idioms** — Boers, Eyckmans & Stengers (2007, *Language Teaching Research*, https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1362168806072460): telling learners the literal origin of an idiom helped them *infer* its figurative meaning and improved recall, and even helped them judge register; interpreted via levels-of-processing and dual coding (the origin evokes imagery). Earlier studies in the series (Boers 2001; Boers, Demecheleer & Eyckmans 2004) are `NOT VERIFIED` this session. `STRONG within idioms; generalisation to ordinary words is an inference.`
- **Semantic vs mnemonic elaboration over time** — Wang & Thomas (1995, *Journal of Educational Psychology*, ERIC EJ517169, https://eric.ed.gov/?q=%22Effect+of+Keywords+on+Long-Term+Retention%22): 176 students; the keyword mnemonic won on immediate tests but *semantic-context* learning produced better recall two days later. `STRONG` that shallow mnemonics decay faster than meaning-based elaboration (other studies, e.g. Sagarra & Alba 2006, find keyword advantages for beginners — `MIXED` overall).
- **The counter-finding you must design around** — Barcroft (2002, *Language Learning*, ERIC EJ647300, https://eric.ed.gov/?q=%22Semantic+and+Structural+Elaboration+in+L2+Lexical+Acquisition%22): low-intermediate learners of Spanish learning 24 words; "increased semantic processing can inhibit the ability to encode the formal properties of new words." Elaborate *meaning* work at first exposure steals resources from learning the *form*. `STRONG (replicated in Barcroft's TOPRA line of work).`

**Morphological awareness instruction.** `STRONG.`
- Bowers, Kirby & Deacon (2010, *Review of Educational Research*, https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/0034654309359353): 22 studies, pre-K to grade 8; morphological instruction improved vocabulary, reading and spelling, "brings particular benefits for less able readers," is "no less effective for younger students," and "is more effective when combined with other aspects of literacy instruction."
- Goodwin & Ahn (2013, *Scientific Studies of Reading*, ERIC EJ1012349): 30 studies, 92 effect sizes, overall **d = 0.32**.
These are L1/English-literacy studies, but they are the strongest evidence that teaching roots/affixes as a *system* transfers to new words.

**Loanword strata as a learning resource.** `MIXED→STRONG.`
- English loanwords in Japanese: gairaigo are now 80–90% English-origin; Daulton's studies found they "assist in English-word aural recognition and pronunciation, spelling, listening comprehension, retention ... and recognition and recall at especially higher levels of vocabulary" (summary at https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gairaigo). Daulton's headline figure that roughly half of the 3,000 most frequent English words have common katakana counterparts (Daulton 2008, *Japan's Built-in Lexicon of English-based Loanwords*) is `NOT VERIFIED` this session.
- Shared loanwords beat inherited cognates for transparency: Gooskens, Kürschner & van Heuven 2022 (Section 2.3) — Romance/Greek/English loans >90% recognisable across Danish/Swedish vs 73% for inherited words, because recent loans have not diverged.
- Sino-xenic layer: ~60% of Japanese dictionary entries are kango but only 18–20% of words in speech (NHK 1989 corpus; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sino-Japanese_vocabulary); Sino-Korean is ~57% of the *Standard Korean Language Dictionary* (estimates 30–70%; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sino-Korean_vocabulary), and Japanese-coined kango were borrowed into Korean and Vietnamese, so many modern abstract terms are shared three ways.
- Perso-Arabic layer: Hindustani has ~5,500 words of Persian and Arabic origin, and ~75% of a 55,000-word Urdu stock is Sanskrit/Prakrit-derived (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindustani_language); Turkish retains 6,463 Arabic and 1,374 Persian words (plus 4,974 French) in the TDK dictionary, ~14% foreign overall (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_vocabulary). Bengali is ~40% tatsama Sanskrit with ~16% tadbhava (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tatsama). Shares for Punjabi and for Perso-Arabic vocabulary in Bengali/Punjabi are `NOT VERIFIED`.

## 5. Indian-context family leverage for a Hindi/Konkani native

**Hindi–Urdu.** At the spoken level "registers of a single language" with identical grammar and core vocabulary; divergence is in formal/literary vocabulary (Sanskrit vs Perso-Arabic) and script; "conversion from Hindi to Urdu ... is generally achieved by merely transliterating" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindustani_language). For you the cost is Nastaʿlīq literacy plus the high-register Perso-Arabic layer — which then pays forward into Persian and (via 6,000+ Arabic/Persian words) Turkish. `STRONG.`

**Konkani–Marathi.** Katre (1966) showed by comparative method across six dialects that Konkani formed separately from Marathi, but they are the closest neighbours; Konkani carries a Kannada/Dravidian substrate (question-final markers, copula deletion, phrasal verbs, loans like *naall*, *madval*, *choru*) and is "not highly Sanskritised like Marathi" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Konkani_language). Konkani speakers were 74.2% bilingual and 44.7% trilingual in 1991 (national averages 19.4% / 7.3%) — you are already living the model. Marathi has a very high tatsama load and had heavy Persian vocabulary historically (one source cited on the Marathi page claims 80% Persian vocabulary in 1630 falling to 37% by 1677 — register-specific and `NOT VERIFIED`) (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marathi_language). No published lexical-similarity coefficient for Konkani–Marathi, Hindi–Marathi, Hindi–Gujarati, Hindi–Punjabi or Hindi–Nepali could be retrieved this session (`NOT VERIFIED`); the asymmetric, exposure-driven comprehension (Punjabi/Marathi speakers understand Hindi far better than the reverse) is well known but I have no measured figure.

**Realistic "cheap" targets, in order (my synthesis of the above):**
1. *Urdu* (script + register only) → 2. *Marathi* (same script, shared Sanskrit layer, Konkani-adjacent grammar) → 3. *Nepali* (Devanagari, Sanskrit layer, simpler agreement) → 4. *Gujarati / Punjabi* (new but isomorphic scripts, Hindustani-adjacent) → 5. *Bengali* (Eastern Nagari script, no grammatical gender, ~40% tatsama) → 6. *Telugu / Kannada / Malayalam*: unrelated grammar, but a large Sanskrit tatsama layer (Malayalam "has a substantially high number of Sanskrit loanwords", with Grantha letters added to its script specifically to write them, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malayalam; Telugu absorbed tatsamas over ~500 years, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tatsama). Konkani's Kannada substrate gives you a small structural head-start for Kannada. Tamil's purist vocabulary makes it the least "cheap" of the four.

**Scripts.** All the above except Urdu use Brahmic abugidas that are structurally isomorphic: ISCII "encodes letters with the same phonetic value at the same code point, overlaying the various scripts" (so one code sequence renders as कि / ਕਿ / കി / கி), and Unicode "largely preserves the ISCII layout within each block" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_Script_Code_for_Information_Interchange). This means an app can show any Indic word in Devanagari as a scaffold and fade it out. Direct experimental evidence for Brahmic→Brahmic *reading transfer* (as opposed to the akshara-literacy work of Nag & Snowling) could not be retrieved — `NOT VERIFIED`; treat as a strong structural argument rather than a measured effect.

**Konkani's multi-script situation** — Devanagari (official in Goa since 1987; Hindus), Roman/Romi (Catholics; oldest literature, 16th c.), Kannada script (Karnataka), Malayalam script (Kerala, now shifting to Devanagari), Perso-Arabic (Konkani Muslims) (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Konkani_language) — is itself a live demonstration that script is a thin layer over a shared phonology, which is the pedagogical message your app wants to send.

**Portuguese loanwords.** In Hindustani: *anannās* < ananás, *pādrī* < padre, *bālṭī* < balde, *chābī* < chave, *girjā* < igreja, *almārī* < armário, *botal* < botelha, *aspatāl* < hospital (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindustani_etymology). In Konkani the Portuguese layer is concentrated in Catholic dialects (religious and everyday vocabulary), limited in Hindu dialects (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Konkani_language). Marathi lists (batātā, pāv, kāju etc.) and Dalgado's 1913 *Influência do vocabulário português em línguas asiáticas* are `NOT VERIFIED` this session. These words are a ready-made bridge from Konkani/Hindi into Portuguese and, via Portuguese, into Spanish.

## 6. The learner's own case: Spanish-elementary and Japanese-elementary

**Adding Portuguese / Italian / Catalan to elementary Spanish.**
- Lexical similarity (Ethnologue coefficients via https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lexical_similarity): Spanish–Portuguese 0.89, Spanish–Catalan ≈0.85–0.87 (table read as 87%; printed Ethnologue value I recall as 85% — `flag`), Spanish–Italian 0.82, Spanish–French 0.75; Catalan–Italian 0.85–0.87, Catalan–Portuguese 0.86. Lexical similarity ≠ intelligibility (phonology/morphology matter).
- Listening (Gooskens 2017): Spanish listeners understand Italian 45.7% and Portuguese 37.2% untrained; Portuguese listeners get Spanish at 77.4%. Reading is markedly easier than listening (Delsing & Lundin Åkesson; intercomprehension programmes are text-first).
- Interference: Spanish will be the *default supplier* into Portuguese regardless of order (Carvalho & da Silva 2006), and elementary Spanish is precisely the proficiency band at which L2→L3 leakage is strongest (Wang 2011 review). Gil's restructuring problem applies to Portuguese (subtle differences in subjunctive/future, article contractions, nasal vowels).
- **Practical reading of the evidence:** receptive Portuguese/Italian/Catalan via the seven sieves is cheap now; *productive* Portuguese before Spanish is at least B1 is the single most predictable place for portuñol-type fossilisation. Italian is the safer second productive Romance target (lower overlap = fewer blends, per the loanword/inherited-cognate logic) — an inference, `MIXED`.

**Japanese and Korean.** Grammar: both SOV, agglutinative, topic-prominent, null-subject, with light-verb nominalisation (*suru*/*hada*) and the two "most elaborate honorific systems" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparison_of_Japanese_and_Korean). Genetic relationship unproven (Altaic discredited; Transeurasian contested; a 2016 paper proposes ~500 shared core items). Vocabulary: the Sino-xenic layer (kango ≈60% of Japanese dictionary entries, 18–20% of speech; Sino-Korean ≈57% of the Korean dictionary) plus Japanese-coined kango shared by both. So Japanese→Korean is a "pseudo-family" on two independent axes: typology and a shared loan stratum. The widely reported fact that Korean-L1 learners are the fastest Japanese learners (and vice versa) is `NOT VERIFIED` by a citation this session. Gil (2026) is the caution: Japanese learners of Korean were *outperformed* by English learners on wh-indefinites — closeness cheapens vocabulary and gross syntax, not the fine print.

**Does a shared loanword stratum work like a family?** Yes for reception and vocabulary, on the evidence available: Gooskens et al. 2022 (recent shared loans are *more* transparent than inherited cognates), Daulton (katakana English aids Japanese learners of English), EuroCom's first sieve (internationalisms), and the ISCII/Devanagari point for Indic scripts. `MIXED→STRONG for vocabulary; no evidence it helps grammar.`

## 7. Design principles the evidence supports, and the biggest risks

### Principles
1. **Receptive-first, family-wide; productive one-at-a-time.** Offer reading/listening in a whole family (seven-sieves style), but gate *speaking/writing* in a second sibling until the first sibling is solid (~B1 as a practitioner heuristic). (Intercomprehension programmes; Ringbom 2007; Marx 2012; Carvalho & da Silva 2006.)
2. **Pair a productive target with a distant productive target, not a sibling.** Concurrent Spanish + Japanese production is evidence-compatible (Rahmatian & Farshadjou 2013; Huang et al. 2022); Spanish + Portuguese production is not.
3. **Interleave languages within a session, with spacing — and give each language a distinct sensory identity** (voice, colour, script, layout). Interleaving two languages helped, apparently via spacing (Libersky et al. 2025); distinct indexical cues are the standard remedy for cross-language competition (Williams & Hammarberg; Weiss, Gerfen & Mitchel 2009 on dual artificial-language segmentation — `NOT VERIFIED` this session).
4. **Make transfer explicit and directional.** Show the learner *which* known language will leak into the new one (closest/most recent non-native: Rothman 2011; Bardel & Falk 2007; Kellerman) and pre-teach the contrasts at exactly those points ("profile words", article contractions, false friends).
5. **Teach cognates as a strategy, not just a list.** Learners find only ~half of cognates unaided; instruction, morphological transparency and metalinguistic skill predict use (Nagy et al. 1993; Dressler et al. 2011). Include suffix-mapping rules (*-dad/-dade/-tà/-ty*; Sanskrit *-tā/-tva* across Indic).
6. **Sequence elaboration: form first, story second.** Present the word form with minimal semantic load at first exposure, then unlock etymology/loanword history at the second or third retrieval (Barcroft 2002 vs Boers et al. 2007; Wang & Thomas 1995). Etymology is a *retention* and *inference* tool, not a first-contact tool.
7. **Build morphology as a system across the family** (roots, affixes, sound correspondences) — the best-evidenced "thoughtful" component (Bowers et al. 2010; Goodwin & Ahn 2013), especially for weaker learners.
8. **Exploit loanword strata as pseudo-families**: Perso-Arabic (Urdu→Persian→Turkish), Sanskrit tatsama (Indic + Dravidian), Portuguese in Indic, Sino-xenic (Japanese↔Korean), internationalisms everywhere. Recent shared loans are more transparent than deep cognates (Gooskens et al. 2022).
9. **Use Devanagari as a transliteration scaffold for all Brahmic-script targets and fade it** (ISCII/Unicode isomorphism), and treat Konkani's five scripts as the worked example.
10. **Split "receptive" into reading vs listening and set expectations per pair** using measured intelligibility (Spanish→Portuguese listening 37% vs reading much higher; Hindi→Urdu spoken ≈100%).
11. **Track variability, not just gains.** Concurrent learners showed more fluctuation in production fluency (Huang et al. 2022); surface it as normal rather than as failure.
12. **Preserve the bilingual-advantage precondition: keep the native languages literate and active.** The multilingual advantage is conditional on additive contexts and literacy (Cenoz 2003; Dolas et al. 2022); Konkani in particular is declining (−9.3% speakers 2001–2011), and an app that keeps it in the loop as a *source* language is both pedagogically and culturally sound.

### Biggest risks (with the study behind each)
| Risk | Evidence |
|---|---|
| **Sibling-language blends and fossilised hybrids** when producing two close languages early (portuñol) | Lipski 2006; Carvalho & da Silva 2006; De Angelis & Selinker 2001; Williams & Hammarberg 1998 |
| **Assumed similarity → wrong meanings persist** (false friends are *easy to remember* and hard to correct) | Ringbom 2007; Marecka et al. 2021 |
| **Closeness makes fine grammar harder, not easier** (restructuring vs development) | Gil 2026 |
| **Etymology/story overload at first exposure suppresses form learning** | Barcroft 2002 |
| **Mnemonic-style cleverness that tests well today and decays** | Wang & Thomas 1995 |
| **Over-promising from intelligibility figures**: 40% cloze is a threshold, not fluency; listening lags reading; asymmetries are large | Gooskens et al. 2017; Delsing & Lundin Åkesson 2005 |
| **Programme claims without outcome data** (EuRom5 "35–40 h") — do not copy the marketing | Bonvino et al. 2018; Baraldi & Santoro 2020 |
| **Cognates silently ignored by learners** unless taught | Nagy et al. 1993 |
| **Extra variability/instability in the weaker language** during concurrent study | Huang et al. 2022 |
| **Thin evidence base for the exact product**: no controlled study of two sibling languages learned concurrently from zero with productive goals | Sections 1.4–1.5 |
