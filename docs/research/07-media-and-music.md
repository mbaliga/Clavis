# 07 — Media & Music: Does Multi-Media Exposure "Ignite Recall"? Evidence + Openly-Licensed Sources

*Research memo for an open-source 9-language app (es fr it de nl ja ko zh ar) built on spaced micro-encounters (Pepper&Carrot parallel comics, device TTS, games). Date: 2026-08-31. Source tags: **[fetched]** = page/PDF opened and read; **[search-snippet]** = seen only in search results (bibliographic identity confirmed, content not independently read).*

## Part 1 — Evidence

### 1a. Music, songs, and verbal memory

**Ludke, Ferreira & Overy (2014), *Memory & Cognition*** — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13421-013-0342-5) [search-snippet]; [open PDF, Ferreira Lab](https://ferreiralab.faculty.ucdavis.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/222/2015/05/Ludke-et-al.-2014_SingingForeignLanguage_Mem-Cog.pdf) [fetched]. **n = 60** adults, randomized to *speaking*, *rhythmic speaking*, or *singing* while learning 20 English–Hungarian paired phrases in 15 min of listen-and-repeat. Singing beat speaking on verbatim Hungarian production, *t*(38) = 2.38, **d = 0.75**, and beat rhythmic speaking on a delayed Hungarian "conversation" test, *t*(38) = 3.01, **d = 0.95**; no group differences on recognition, multiple-choice vocabulary, or English recall. Takeaway: a *sung, imitated* presentation helps **verbatim production** of connected phrases — exactly the skill a phrase-based app targets — but not recognition-style knowledge.

**Wallace (1994), *JEP: Learning, Memory, and Cognition* 20(6), 1471–1485** — [Semantic Scholar record](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Memory-for-music:-Effect-of-melody-on-recall-of-Wallace/bbd36596cf39f88c1f3066526a96561b4768f484) [search-snippet]; summarized in detail in an open ERIC review of melodic mnemonics ([ED504997 PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED504997.pdf) [fetched]). Four experiments with ballad verses: when the **same simple melody repeated across three verses**, sung text was recalled better than spoken text; when **each verse carried a different melody**, sung recall fell *below* spoken; a single sung hearing was also worse than spoken. Wallace's own framing: "Music is a rich structure that chunks words and phrases, identifies line lengths, identifies stress patterns, and adds emphasis" — but only once the melody itself is learned. **This is the central boundary condition**: melody is a scaffold only if it is simple, repeated, and already familiar; otherwise it is an extra load.

**Salcedo (2010), *Journal of College Teaching & Learning* 7(6)** — [open PDF, Wellesley mirror](https://www1.wellesley.edu/sites/default/files/assets/departments/pltc/files/faculty/salcedo_2010_songs_in_foreign_language_classrooms.pdf) [fetched]; [ERIC EJ895263](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ895263) [search-snippet]. **n = 94** beginning Spanish undergraduates; cloze recall of texts studied as songs vs. spoken versions vs. control. Immediate recall favored the song group for 2 of 3 songs (p = .0504, p = .0345); **delayed recall (2 weeks) showed no group difference** (F = 0.94, p = .40); but self-reported **involuntary mental rehearsal ("the din") occurred in 66.7% of song learners vs 33.3% of text learners** (χ² = 7.22, p < .03). The "earworm" mechanism the founder is gesturing at is real and measurable — songs get replayed in the head unbidden — but by itself it did not preserve a recall advantage at 2 weeks.

**Medina (1990/1993)** — [ERIC ED352834](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED352834) [search-snippet]. Early small study (n = 48 second-grade ESL learners; sung vs. spoken story, with/without illustrations): trends favored sung + illustrated presentation for vocabulary gain but the study was underpowered; cited here for historical completeness, not as strong evidence.

**Good, Russo & Sullivan (2015), *Psychology of Music*** — [Sage](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0305735614528833) [fetched abstract]. Spanish-speaking Ecuadorian children learned an English passage over 2 weeks as a **song vs. an oral poem**. The sung group outperformed on **verbatim recall, English vowel pronunciation, and English→Spanish translation of target words**, and "the song advantage persevered after a 6-month delay." One of the few studies showing durable transfer to meaning (translation), in children, with a repeated melody.

**Pavia, Webb & Faez (2019), *Studies in Second Language Acquisition* 41(4)** — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/abs/incidental-vocabulary-learning-through-listening-to-songs/24418DF071558AE40C2D429B6868F8B9) [search-snippet]; [ERIC EJ1228203](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1228203) [fetched abstract]. EFL learners listened to two songs **1, 3, or 5 times**; incidental gains measured for spoken-form recognition, form–meaning connection, and collocation recognition. Results: listening to songs produced vocabulary learning; **repeated listening and higher in-song frequency of the target items both increased gains**. This is the best direct evidence for the app's "encounter the same item repeatedly inside a song" use case.

**Systematic review of song interventions** — "Investigating the substantive linguistic effects of using songs for teaching second or foreign languages to preschool, primary and secondary school learners," *System* (2024), [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0346251X24001325) [search-snippet; robots-blocked]. Reviews the intervention literature on songs for young learners; the field's general pattern (echoed in the studies above) is positive-but-heterogeneous effects with many weak designs — so treat songs as promising, not proven at scale.

**Boundary conditions (where music hurts):**

- **Background music with lyrics during effortful study impairs performance.** Souza & Barbosa (2023), *Journal of Cognition*, [open access](https://journalofcognition.org/articles/10.5334/joc.273) [fetched]: within-subjects, **n ≈ 113–123** students; **music with lyrics impaired verbal memory (d = −.32), visual memory (d = −.33), and reading comprehension (d = −.19)** relative to silence, while instrumental lo-fi had no credible effect either way. Mechanism: the **irrelevant speech/sound effect** — lyric speech content produces phonological/semantic interference. (Participants correctly judged lyrics harmful but wrongly believed instrumental music helped.)
- **Meta-analytically, background music is at best neutral for learning.** de la Mora Velasco et al. (2023), "The impact of background music on learners: a systematic review and meta-analysis," *Psychology of Music*, [Sage](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/03057356231153070) [search-snippet].
- **Seductive details (interesting-but-irrelevant media) reliably hurt learning.** Sundararajan & Adesope (2020), "Keep it Coherent: A Meta-Analysis of the Seductive Details Effect," *Educational Psychology Review*, [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-020-09522-4) [search-snippet]; consistent with Mayer's **coherence principle** — first shown for background music/sounds in multimedia lessons by Moreno & Mayer (2000), "A coherence effect in multimedia learning," *JEP* 92(1), [APA record](https://psycnet.apa.org/doi/10.1037/0022-0663.92.1.117) [search-snippet]. Adding entertaining audio *around* the lesson lowered retention and transfer.
- **Wallace's melody caveat** (above): unrepeated or verse-varying melodies flip the benefit to a cost.

### 1b. Multi-modal and varied encounters

**Dual coding.** Clark & Paivio (1991), "Dual coding theory and education," *Educational Psychology Review* 3, 149–210, [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF01320076) [search-snippet]. Verbal + imagistic codes are additive; a word encoded with a picture gets two retrieval routes. This is the theoretical basis for the app's comic bubbles and for adding photos **that depict the meaning of the target phrase** (referential connections), not decorative media.

**Multimedia principle, with limits.** Mayer's program shows words + pictures beat words alone, but the same program supplies the coherence/redundancy limits above: extra media must be *relevant and integrated*, or it subtracts. (Moreno & Mayer 2000 [search-snippet], Sundararajan & Adesope 2020 [search-snippet], as cited in 1a.)

**Encoding variability — mixed, but modern support for meaningful variation.** Maddox (2016), "Understanding the underlying mechanism of the spacing effect in verbal learning: a case for encoding variability and study-phase retrieval," *Journal of Cognitive Psychology*, [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/303558100_Understanding_the_underlying_mechanism_of_the_spacing_effect_in_verbal_learning_a_case_for_encoding_variability_and_study-phase_retrieval) [search-snippet]: reviews evidence that classic "different superficial context at each repetition" manipulations often fail, and that study-phase retrieval (each new encounter re-activating the old trace) carries much of the spacing effect. But newer large studies find benefits when the *mnemonic content* varies: Feng et al. (2024), "The effects of mnemonic variability and spacing on memory over multiple timescales," *PNAS*, [PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/abs/10.1073/pnas.2311077121) [search-snippet]; and "Variation in encoding context benefits item recognition," [PMC open access](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12053356/) [search-snippet]. Practical reading: vary the **semantic context and media** around a word across encounters, while keeping the word-form and meaning constant — do not rely on cosmetic variation.

**Contextual diversity beats raw frequency.** Adelman, Brown & Quesada (2006), *Psychological Science*: contextual diversity, not word frequency, determines word-naming and lexical-decision times — [Sage](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01787.x) [search-snippet]; [open Warwick repository copy](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/399/) [search-snippet]. Johns, Dye & Jones (2016) extended this to learning: words encountered across more distinct contexts are processed/learned better, [Springer, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-015-0980-7) [search-snippet]. Direct experimental evidence, including foreign-language learning:

- **Frances, Martín & Duñabeitia (2020), *Scientific Reports*** — [open access](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-020-70922-1) [fetched]. **n = 88**; new (pseudo)words appeared **8 times total in 1, 2, 4, or 8 different texts**, in L1 Spanish or L2 English. More texts → better recall (F(3,258) = 13.71, ηp² = .14), recognition (ηp² = .11), and meaning-matching (ηp² = .04), **with identical benefits in the foreign language**. Same number of exposures, spread over more contexts, produced more learning.
- **Rosa, Tapia & Perea (2017), *PLOS ONE*** — [open access](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0179004) [fetched]. **n = 33** third-graders; 12 new words appeared 3 times either in **three different genres (fable, science text, math problem) or one genre**. High-diversity words won on all four measures (free recall, recognition, multiple choice, picture matching). Replicated in Rosa et al. (2022), *JECP*, "regardless of comprehension skills," [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0022096521002307) [search-snippet].

**Physical/environmental context variation.** Smith, Glenberg & Bjork (1978), *Memory & Cognition* 6, 342–353: lists studied in **two different rooms** were recalled better than the same list studied twice in one room — [Springer](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03197465) [search-snippet]. The meta-analysis: Smith & Vela (2001), "Environmental context-dependent memory: a review and meta-analysis," *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 8, 203–220 — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03196157) [search-snippet]; [open PDF (author site)](https://people.it.tamu.edu/~stevesmith/SmithMemory/Smith&Vela2001.pdf) [search-snippet]. Context effects are reliable but small, and shrink when tests provide strong cues — so context variation is a cheap add-on, not a main lever.

### 1c. The "tingle": familiarity without recollection, and whether pre-exposure pays off later

The founder's phenomenon — "a phrase glimpsed in film credits later tingles memory" — is **familiarity without recollection**, and it is one of the best-documented dissociations in memory science.

- **Dual-process recognition.** Yonelinas (2002), "The nature of recollection and familiarity: a review of 30 years of research," *Journal of Memory and Language* 46, 441–517 — [ScienceDirect/DOI](https://doi.org/10.1006/jmla.2002.2864) [search-snippet]. Familiarity is a fast, graded signal independent of recollecting the source — the "tingle" is a real memory signal, not an illusion.
- **Incidental exposure leaves enormous traces.** Standing (1973): after viewing **10,000 pictures**, recognition remained far above chance — *QJEP* 25, 207–222, [Sage](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1080/14640747308400340) [search-snippet]. Brady, Konkle, Alvarez & Oliva (2008), *PNAS* 105(38): after **2,500 objects** seen once each, forced-choice recognition was **87–92% even for exemplar and state details** — [PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/abs/10.1073/pnas.0803390105) [search-snippet]; [open PDF](https://konklab.fas.harvard.edu/Papers/Brady_2008_PNAS.pdf) [search-snippet]. Passive exposure is far from wasted.
- **Savings in relearning: "forgotten" is not "gone."** Nelson (1978), "Detecting small amounts of information in memory: savings for nonrecalled items," *JEP: Human Learning and Memory* 4, 453–468 — [APA record](https://psycnet.apa.org/doi/10.1037/0278-7393.4.5.453) [search-snippet]: items that could be neither recalled nor recognized were still **relearned faster** than new items. MacLeod (1988), "Forgotten but not gone: savings for pictures and words in long-term memory," *JEP: LMC* 14, 195–212 — [APA record](https://psycnet.apa.org/doi/10.1037/0278-7393.14.2.195) [search-snippet]. Most strikingly for language: Bowers, Mattys & Gage (2009), *Psychological Science*, "Preserved implicit knowledge of a forgotten childhood language" — adults with early, long-lost exposure to Hindi or Zulu **relearned its phoneme contrasts better than controls despite no explicit memory** — [Sage](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2009.02407.x) [search-snippet]; [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19645694/) [search-snippet].
- **Fluency and mere exposure.** Prior exposure speeds later perceptual processing even without recognition (Jacoby & Dallas 1981, *JEP: General* 110, 306–340 — [APA record](https://psycnet.apa.org/doi/10.1037/0096-3445.110.3.306) [search-snippet]); mere exposure raises liking, robustly and even for unrecognized stimuli (Zajonc 1968 — [APA record](https://psycnet.apa.org/doi/10.1037/h0025848) [search-snippet]; Bornstein 1989 meta-analysis — [APA record](https://psycnet.apa.org/doi/10.1037/0033-2909.106.2.265) [search-snippet]). Liking the material matters for an app that lives on voluntary return visits.
- **Does incidental pre-exposure speed later *intentional* L2 word learning? Yes, measurably.** Elgort et al. (2024), "Previewing novel words before reading affects their processing during reading," *Language Learning* — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/lang.12579) [search-snippet; publisher 403'd on fetch]: brief pre-exposure to unfamiliar word forms changed how L1 and L2 readers processed those words on later encounters in text (eye-movement evidence of a head start). The older "partial word knowledge" literature agrees: people reliably hold graded, below-threshold knowledge of "frontier" words they claim not to know (Durso & Shore 1991, *JEP: LMC* 17, 190–202 — [APA record](https://psycnet.apa.org/doi/10.1037/0278-7393.17.2.190) [search-snippet]). And in songs specifically, Pavia et al. (2019) (above, [fetched]) shows *purely incidental* listening produces measurable form-recognition gains — the first rung of the ladder.

**Interpretation for the app:** low-effort media encounters (a lyric heard, a photo caption glimpsed) plausibly do three real things: (1) build **form familiarity** that later shows up as savings/faster relearning; (2) create **perceptual fluency and liking** that keep users engaged; (3) leave **partial knowledge** that intentional study can complete faster. What they do *not* do reliably is produce recallable form–meaning knowledge on their own — that still requires the app's explicit spaced encounters.

### 1d. Verdict

**Where multi-media genuinely helps.** (i) *Songs whose lyrics are the target*, with simple melodies repeated verbatim across encounters: gains in verbatim phrase production and pronunciation (Ludke 2014 [fetched]; Good et al. 2015 [fetched]; Wallace 1994 [fetched-via-review]), plus free involuntary rehearsal (Salcedo 2010 [fetched]) and incidental vocabulary pickup that scales with repetitions (Pavia et al. 2019 [fetched]). (ii) *Varied meaningful contexts for the same item*: the contextual-diversity results (Frances 2020 [fetched]; Rosa 2017 [fetched]; Adelman 2006 [search-snippet]) are the strongest scientific support for the founder's instinct — meeting *the same word* in a comic, a song line, a photo caption and a news clip beats four repetitions of one flashcard, at equal exposure counts. (iii) *Pictures that depict the meaning*, per dual coding (Clark & Paivio 1991 [search-snippet]). (iv) *Incidental pre-exposure* as a deliberate design layer: cheap familiarity now, faster explicit learning later (Elgort 2024; Nelson 1978; Bowers 2009 — [search-snippet]).

**Where it hurts.** (i) Background music — especially with lyrics in *any* language — playing **during** effortful exercises (Souza & Barbosa 2023 [fetched]; de la Mora Velasco 2023 [search-snippet]). (ii) Decorative, interesting-but-irrelevant media attached to lessons (seductive-details meta [search-snippet]; Moreno & Mayer 2000 [search-snippet]). (iii) Melodies that change per verse, or one-shot sung presentations (Wallace 1994). (iv) Cosmetic variation that changes the surface but not the semantic context (Maddox 2016 [search-snippet]).

**Design rules.** 1) Media is a *channel for the target item*, never wallpaper: no ambient soundtrack during games or SRS reviews. 2) Songs: short, simple, strophic melodies; same melody every repetition; lyrics = target phrases; show parallel text. 3) Schedule the *same* item across *different* media (comic → song line → photo caption → spoken sentence) inside the existing spaced-repetition engine — variability in context, constancy in form/meaning. 4) Treat passive feeds (a lyric of the day, a 15-second clip) as **pre-exposure**, and route pre-exposed items into explicit study a few days later, where savings are harvested. 5) Every picture must depict the phrase's meaning; drop any media that fails that test.

### Practical verdict — ingest order

1. **Mozilla Common Voice (CC0)** first: one license, zero attribution burden, all 9 languages, sentence-level clip+text pairs that drop directly into micro-encounters and games; bulk pipeline already exists (Data Collective downloads/API [fetched]).
2. **LibriVox + Wikimedia Commons audio (PD / per-file PD-CC)** second: LibriVox gives deep listening material for es/fr/de/nl/it (thousands of hours) and *some* ja/zh; Commons fills words (Lingua Libre), anthems, and folk-song recordings with machine-readable per-file licenses — filter to `PD`/`CC0`/`CC BY`/`CC BY-SA` at ingest.
3. **For actual songs with vocals**: start with **Commons "Songs by language" + PD-composition folk songs re-sung in-house or via community (melody from IMSLP/Musopen, lyrics PD)** — this is the only path that is simultaneously licensing-clean in all markets, since (a) Great 78 material ≤1925 is PD **in the US only** and must be geo-assessed for an app distributed worldwide, and (b) FMA vocal tracks need track-by-track NC/ND filtering. Commissioning simple strophic recordings of PD folk lyrics also happens to satisfy the science's main constraint (simple, repeated melody carrying the target text — Wallace 1994).

**Bottom line for the founder:** the evidence supports the *spirit* of the proposal — varied, multi-media encounters with the *same* items measurably beat single-context repetition, and incidental exposure leaves real, harvestable traces ("the tingle" = familiarity + savings). It does not support media as ambience: anything not carrying the target phrase — background music during exercises, decorative clips, per-verse melodies — has documented negative effects. Build media as *scheduled contexts inside the SRS*, not as atmosphere.
