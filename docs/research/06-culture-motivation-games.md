# Culture-Integrated Language Learning, Motivation, Micro-Games, Scripts and Multi-Language Display: Evidence Review

*Research brief for a "Busuu × GeoGuessr" language app. Compiled 2026-08-29. Evidence tags: **STRONG** = meta-analysis/systematic review or replicated experiments; **MIXED** = contested, small-N, or confounded; **ANECDOTAL** = practitioner/industry data, case studies, theory without direct tests. Where a figure is quoted from memory of a paper that could not be re-fetched in this session it is marked "(verify)".*

---

## 1. Learning language through content and culture

### 1.1 CLIL / content-based instruction — what is robust

- **Graham, Choi, Davoodi, Razmeh & Dixon (2018), *LACLIL* — systematic review of 25 CLIL/EMI studies (2008–2018).** Most studies show positive-or-neutral language outcomes for content-based instruction; speaking (pronunciation, negotiation) shows more consistent gains than writing; receptive vocabulary advantages in 3 of 6 studies (all primary). Content outcomes: positive in maths, negative in science "particularly for low-ability students". Authors flag selection bias, confounded instruction time, and lack of pre-tests. [PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1190990.pdf) — **MIXED, leaning positive for language.**
- **Goris, Denessen & Verhoeven (2019), *European Educational Research Journal* — systematic review of 21 longitudinal experimental CLIL studies in Europe.** Receptive vocabulary (the most-tested skill) showed *no significant longitudinal growth advantage* in most Dutch, Swedish and Austrian studies; oral fluency was "the most favourably affected" skill (Spain); large gains (d > 1.0) appear mainly in Pérez-Cañado's 2,024-learner study. Central caveat: CLIL "selects or attracts high-achieving students" and control groups are often negatively selected. [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/1474904119872426) — **MIXED.**
- **EMI-CLIL multilevel meta-analysis (2023; 38 studies, 44 samples, N = 7,434).** Pooled d = 0.73 at post-test and d = 1.01 at delayed post-test; *vocabulary shows the largest effects*, productive skills (speaking/writing) the smallest (β = −0.34). Effects are larger when L1 is related to English and, tellingly, larger in studies that did *not* confirm baseline equivalence — direct evidence of selection bias inflating the literature. [PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1397295.pdf) — **STRONG for vocabulary/receptive gains, with a selection-bias discount.**
- **Dalton-Puffer (2011), *Annual Review of Applied Linguistics* 31.** The canonical narrative review: CLIL gains are consistent for receptive skills, vocabulary, morphology, fluency and affect; weak or absent for syntax, writing accuracy, pragmatics and pronunciation. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0267190511000092) — **narrative, widely replicated pattern.**
- **Goris, Denessen & Verhoeven (2019), *Language Learning Journal* — "The contribution of CLIL to learners' international orientation and EFL confidence."** CLIL cohorts score higher on international orientation and confidence, but the same authors' review shows initial group differences are rarely controlled. [ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1309617) — **MIXED.**

**Take-away for the app:** teaching a country's geography/history *through* the language should reliably grow receptive vocabulary and confidence, and is unlikely to hurt; do not expect it to deliver grammar accuracy or production on its own. Add explicit production tasks.

### 1.2 Narrow (topic-bound) reading

- **Kang (2015), *RELC Journal* 46(2).** 61 high-intermediate learners; one month of narrow (single-topic) vs wide (unrelated) reading. Narrow reading "considerably facilitated" understanding and appropriate use of target words. [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0033688215586236) — **MIXED (single study, positive).**
- **Schmitt & Carter (2000), *TESOL Journal* 9(1)** and **Krashen (2004, *Language Magazine*, "The case for narrow reading")** argue the mechanism: topic-bound texts recycle low-frequency topical vocabulary and build background knowledge, so each successive text is easier. Several later classroom studies (e.g. an ESP narrow-reading study in *LEARN Journal*, 2025, [PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1479905.pdf)) report better comprehension and vocabulary. — **MIXED-to-STRONG for vocabulary recycling; no meta-analysis exists.**

**Design implication:** "One country = one theme" is a natural narrow-reading design. Its power comes from *repetition of the same content words across many short items* (a Kerala unit that keeps re-using backwaters, coconut, monsoon, Onam), not from the culture per se.

### 1.3 Cultural schema and elaborative encoding

- **Steffensen, Joag-Dev & Anderson (1979), *Reading Research Quarterly* 15(1).** Indian and American readers read letters about an Indian and an American wedding: each recalled more, read faster, produced more culturally appropriate elaborations and fewer distortions for their *own* culture's text. [JSTOR](https://www.jstor.org/stable/747429) — **STRONG (classic, repeatedly replicated).** Replication with Turkish EFL learners: nativised texts scored ~79 vs ~60 for originals, and pre-reading activities could *not* compensate for cultural unfamiliarity (Erten & Razı 2009, *Reading in a Foreign Language*, [PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ838389.pdf)).
  - *Important nuance for the product:* the schema effect says **unfamiliar** cultural content is *harder* to process. Teaching culture first (the flag, the food, the festival) and then the language *builds the schema* that later makes the language input comprehensible; the ordering matters.
- **Craik & Tulving (1975), *JEP: General* 104(3)** — deep semantic processing beats shallow processing (levels of processing). — **STRONG (foundational).**
- **Self-reference effect:** Symons & Johnson (1997) meta-analysis, *Psychological Bulletin* 121(3) — self-referent encoding beats semantic encoding ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9136641/)). Extended to L2 vocabulary by **Pruss, Karni & Prior (2025), *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 32: 2104–2113**: writing a sentence relating a rare word to oneself beat translation production (d = 0.71 immediate) and beat semantic definition (η²p = 0.11), with the advantage surviving one week. [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-025-02674-w) — **STRONG (meta-analysis) + fresh L2 evidence.**
- **Etymological elaboration:** Boers, Demecheleer & Eyckmans (2004, in *Vocabulary in a Second Language*, Benjamins) showed that hypothesising about an idiom's origin improves retention via imagery/dual coding. — **MIXED (idioms only, small samples)**, but the best available support for "word graphs" as an *encoding* device rather than mere decoration.

## 2. Motivation: who learns for culture, and does it predict persistence?

### 2.1 Theory and its predictive power

- **Masgoret & Gardner (2003), *Language Learning* 53(1)** — meta-analysis of 75 samples (~10,500 learners) from Gardner's programme. Motivation correlates with achievement at r ≈ .37; integrativeness ≈ .24; integrative and instrumental *orientations* only ≈ .20 / .16 (verify exact values). [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/1467-9922.00227) — **STRONG**: *wanting to belong to the culture* matters mainly insofar as it feeds sustained motivated behaviour; the label "integrative vs instrumental" is itself a weak predictor.
- **Al-Hoorie (2018), *SSLLT* 8(4)** — meta-analysis of Dörnyei's L2 Motivational Self System: 32 reports, 39 samples, N = 32,078. Ideal L2 self correlates .61 with *intended effort* but only **.20 with objective achievement** (≈4% of variance); ought-to self −.05; learning experience .66 / .17. [PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1202469.pdf) — **STRONG, and cautionary**: self-report motivation inflates apparent effects; measure behaviour.
- **Yashima (2002), *Modern Language Journal* 86(1)** — "international posture" (interest in foreign affairs, readiness to interact with non-Japanese) predicts motivation, which predicts proficiency, and predicts willingness to communicate (N = 297 Japanese students). [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1540-4781.00136) — **MIXED-to-STRONG (well replicated in Japan; cross-sectional).**

**Bottom line:** cultural interest is a real but modest predictor; the *learning experience itself* (r = .66 with effort) is the strongest lever an app controls.

### 2.2 Duolingo's learner-motivation data (industry, self-selected surveys — **ANECDOTAL/observational**)

- **2021 Global Report** ([blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/2021-duolingo-language-report/)): family overtook school and brain-training as the #1 motivator for new US learners; culture is the primary reason for 18% of Japanese learners vs 8–10% for English/Spanish/French; a consumer survey found 65% would rank "learning about a new culture" as top reason; 70% of pandemic-era learners tied learning to heritage/ancestry/culture.
- **Understudied-language post (2021)** ([blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/understudied-language-motivations-2021/)): Yiddish 34% culture, 54% family+culture; Navajo ~28% culture; Hawaiian ~20%; Spanish family ~23%. For English/Spanish/French, school and work dominate (≈19–30%) with culture and family each under 10%.
- **2023 Report** ([investor release](https://investors.duolingo.com/news-releases/news-release-details/duolingo-language-report-reveals-changes-top-10-languages)): 49% of English learners study for education/work; Portuguese learners cite connecting with people (23%) and travel (18%).
- **2024 Report** ([blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/2024-duolingo-language-report/)): for English, education is #1 globally; for Spanish, "connecting with people" is #1 and entertainment #2; South Korea: a third learn English to connect, 23% for education. **India** (Storyboard18 coverage of the India cut, [link](https://www.storyboard18.com/how-it-works/71-of-indian-learners-excited-about-emerging-technology-duolingo-report-49381.htm)): career growth 55%, travel & cultural exploration 45%, global social interaction 42%, entertainment 40%; 46% of Indian respondents frequently switch between languages in conversation; the top obstacle is irregular practice (55%) and cross-language confusion of grammar/vocabulary. India is #1 for Duolingo English Test takers; Hindi has 8.4 M active learners globally.
- **Streaks by language** ([blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/boomers-vs-gen-z-duolingo-streaks)): the highest share of unbroken 14-day streaks is among learners of Scottish Gaelic, Welsh and Yiddish — languages studied overwhelmingly for family/cultural connection — and 79% of 50+ learners keep streaks vs 55% of high-schoolers. This is correlational, but it is the best available observational link between heritage/culture motivation and persistence.

**Segment answer:** among English learners (the largest segment, and the Indian majority) exam/career dominates (~50–55%). Among learners of *any other* language, connection/culture/entertainment/travel motives are the plurality (typically 40–60% combined) and school/career a minority. A multi-language, culture-first app targets exactly the second segment — plus the Indian learner who is *also* learning Korean/Japanese/French for cultural reasons.

## 3. Gamification, quiz formats, streaks and time pressure

### 3.1 Does gamification work?

- **Sailer & Homner (2020), *Educational Psychology Review* 32** — 38 publications/40 experiments. Hedges g = 0.49 (cognitive), 0.36 (motivational), 0.25 (behavioural). Combining competition *with collaboration* beats pure competition; game fiction helps behaviour; interventions > 1 month beat one-day ones. **When restricted to rigorous designs, motivational and behavioural effects become non-significant; only cognitive effects survive.** High heterogeneity (I² 51–84%). [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/S10648-019-09498-W) — **STRONG for "small cognitive benefit", WEAK for motivation.**
- **Zhang & Hasim (2023), *Frontiers in Psychology* 13:1030790** — 40 EFL/ESL gamification studies. Most-used elements: feedback (16), points (15), quizzes (15), badges (9), leaderboards (9). Positive vocabulary/engagement findings dominate, but leaderboards "might scare off" lower-ranked learners, benefits are often short-lived novelty effects, and the authors conclude "competition and repetition are not necessary elements". [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.1030790/full) — **MIXED.**
- **Dehghanzadeh et al. (2021), *Computer Assisted Language Learning* 34(7)** — systematic review of gamified ESL; outcomes mostly self-reported engagement; points/badges/leaderboards dominate. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/09588221.2019.1648298) — **MIXED (could not re-fetch; from prior knowledge).**

### 3.2 Rewards, badges, leaderboards and intrinsic motivation

- **Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999), *Psychological Bulletin* 125(6)** — 128 studies. Expected tangible rewards undermine free-choice intrinsic motivation (d = −0.36); engagement-contingent −0.40, completion-contingent −0.36, performance-contingent −0.28; **positive verbal feedback enhances it (d = +0.33)**. [PDF](https://home.ubalt.edu/tmitch/642/articles%20syllabus/Deci%20Koestner%20Ryan%20meta%20IM%20psy%20bull%2099.pdf) — **STRONG.**
- **Hanus & Fox (2015), *Computers & Education* 80** — 16-week classroom experiment; badges + leaderboard + coins produced *lower* intrinsic motivation, satisfaction and empowerment over time and lower final-exam scores, mediated by motivation. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360131514002000) — **MIXED (one well-designed study; widely cited).**

### 3.3 Quiz format, feedback and difficulty

- **Kang, McDermott & Roediger (2007), *European Journal of Cognitive Psychology* 19(4–5)** — short-answer practice tests beat multiple-choice for delayed retention regardless of final-test format (d ≈ 0.41 with feedback); feedback helped SA but not MC. [Summary](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/zTxLkeaWCdBHQYW73o5n6Ka) — **STRONG (testing-effect literature).**
- **Butler & Roediger (2008), *Memory & Cognition* 36(3)** — without feedback, MC lures create "false knowledge" that intrudes on later recall; immediate *or* delayed feedback both restore correct answers and cut intrusions (no advantage for immediate). [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/MC.36.3.604) — **STRONG.** *Never ship a rapid-fire MC round without showing the correct answer.*
- **Nakata (2016), *IRAL* 54(3)** — 64 learners, 60 Swahili–English pairs on computer flashcards: recall formats beat recognition for productive/orthographic knowledge; recognition suffices when spelling is not required. [De Gruyter](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/iral-2015-0022/html) — **STRONG for L2 vocabulary specifically.**
- **Pyc & Rawson (2009), *Journal of Memory and Language* 60** — the "retrieval effort hypothesis": harder (but successful) retrievals produce better retention. **Bjork & Bjork (2020), *JARMAC* 9(4)** "Desirable difficulties in theory and practice" [PDF](https://www.waddesdonschool.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/Desriable-Difficulties-in-theory-and-practice-Bjork-Bjork-2020.pdf) — **STRONG (large literature).** Too-easy micro-games maximise *performance* and the feeling of fluency while minimising *learning*.
- **Karatas et al. (2025), *Language Teaching Research*** — 17-day Turkish EFL RCT: expanding-interval spaced retrieval with feedback improved delayed accuracy by 18 pp vs 8 pp for massed study; effects driven by high-frequency words. [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/13621688211053525) — **STRONG (small N = 36, but consistent with the spacing meta-literature).**

### 3.4 Time pressure and "rapid-fire" modes

- **Nation (2007), "The four strands", *Innovation in Language Learning and Teaching* 1(1)** — a balanced course spends ~25% on *fluency development*, which requires **easy, already-known material, a message focus, quantity, and time pressure** (4/3/2 speaking, speed reading). Time pressure is for automatising known items, not for first encounters. [PDF](https://www.victoria.ac.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0019/1626121/2007-Four-strands.pdf) — **theory + program evidence.**
- **de Jong & Perfetti (2011), *Language Learning* 61(2)** — 4/3/2 repeated speech under shrinking time limits produced fluency gains (fewer/shorter pauses) that *transferred to new topics*, i.e., proceduralisation. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2010.00620.x) — **MIXED (N = 24; classic).**
- No study directly shows that a *timed recognition* game (Duolingo "Match Madness"-style) improves retention over untimed retrieval; time pressure raises cognitive load and error rate and encourages guessing (theoretical: time-based resource sharing; empirical: retrieval-effort work above). — **ANECDOTAL/theoretical.**
- **Design rule:** untimed effortful recall for new items → spaced, feedback-rich review → *then* timed speed rounds on items already answered correctly ≥2 times.

### 3.5 Streaks (industry data — **ANECDOTAL but large-N**)

- Duolingo Retention PM (Lenny's Podcast, 2024; [summary](https://www.recall.it/summary/business/behind-the-product-duolingo-streaks-or-jackson-shuttleworth-group-pm-retention-team)): ~9 M users hold 365+-day streaks; retention jumps most from day 1→2 and flattens after day 7; giving *two* streak freezes at onboarding beat one, three added nothing; an "earn-back" streak repair was "a retention winner"; the 23.5-hour practice reminder and 10 pm "streak saver" nudges work but are perceived as spammy.
- **Habit science:** Lally et al. (2010), *EJSP* 40 — 96 volunteers, median 66 days to automaticity (range 18–254); **missing one day did not materially reduce automaticity**; only 48% fit the curve ([Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ejsp.674)). Singh et al. (2024), *Healthcare* 12(23):2488 — 20 studies, 2,601 participants: median 59–66 days, mean 106–154; determinants are morning timing, enjoyment, planning and *self-chosen* behaviours ([MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-9032/12/23/2488)). Wood & Neal (2007), *Psychological Review* 114(4) — habits are context-cued; stable cues (time/place/preceding action) matter more than goals ([PDF](https://dornsife.usc.edu/wendy-wood/wp-content/uploads/sites/183/2023/10/wood.neal_.2007psychrev_a_new_look_at_habits_and_the_interface_between_habits_and_goals.pdf)). — **STRONG on duration and cue-dependence.** Fogg's "Tiny Habits" (2019 book; Fogg Behavior Model, *Persuasive* 2009) is a practitioner framework without RCT support — **ANECDOTAL.**
- Streak *anxiety*: no rigorous quantification; Duolingo itself acknowledges the need for "flexibility". A 2025 Oulu master's thesis on streak motivation (couldn't be fetched) and press pieces report "streak-maintaining" minimal lessons. — **ANECDOTAL.** The Lally finding that a missed day barely dents a habit is the scientific justification for forgiving streaks (freezes, weekly streaks, repair).

## 4. Dual coding, imagery and spatial anchors

- **Paivio's dual coding (1971/1986)** and Mayer's multimedia principle are the theoretical basis — **STRONG in general**, but the L2-vocabulary specifics matter:
- **Zhang, Sala & Gobet (2025), *Educational Research Review* 47** — 32 studies, N = 2,056. **L1 translation + picture beats L1-only (g = 0.33) and beats picture-only (g = 0.35) on immediate tests; L1-only and picture-only do not differ; the advantage shrinks at delay (g = 0.27, p = .07).** No moderation by age or receptive/productive task. [LSE eprint](https://eprints.lse.ac.uk/127744/3/1-s2.0-S1747938X25000181-main.pdf) — **STRONG.** *Image-first with no translation (the Drops model) is not better than translation; image + translation is.*
- **Carpenter & Olson (2012), *JEP: LMC* 38(1)** — learning Swahili words with pictures vs English translations: pictures gave no reliable advantage *and* inflated judgments of learning (overconfidence); when learners were warned that pictures create overconfidence, they studied harder and the picture benefit appeared. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21787105/) — **STRONG (replicated pattern).** Design: pair images with recall tests and honest progress meters, not with self-rated mastery.
- **Method of loci:** Dresler et al. (2017), *Neuron* 93(5) — 40 days × 30 min of loci training took novices from ~26–30 to ~62 of 72 words recalled, with +22 words still retained four months later ([EurekAlert summary](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/750131)). — **STRONG for word lists.** For *L2 vocabulary specifically* only small EFL comparisons exist (e.g., rehearsal vs loci in Iranian EFL, [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/282238932_A_Comparative_Study_of_Rehearsal_and_Loci_Methods_in_Learning_Vocabulary_in_EFL_Context)) — **MIXED.**
- **Spatial/environmental context as retrieval cue:** Smith & Vela (2001), *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 8(2) meta-analysis — context reinstatement reliably helps, effect small (d ≈ 0.3, verify), strongest for free recall and when the context is not "outshone" by stronger cues. [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03196157) — **STRONG but small.** Maps, flags and currency can serve as distinctive retrieval cues; the evidence supports using them as *consistent* cues for a word set (the "Kerala map" always accompanies Kerala words), not as decoration that changes each screen.
- **Maps/flags for vocabulary specifically:** no direct experiments found. — **ANECDOTAL** (plausible via loci + context effects).

## 5. Learning scripts and multi-script literacy

- **Taylor, Davis & Rastle (2017), *JEP: General* 146(6)** — 24 adults learned two artificial alphabets (24 words each) over 8 days; **print-to-sound (phonics) training beat print-to-meaning** on reading aloud, comprehension and generalisation to untrained words. [PMC](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5458780) — **STRONG.** Teach letter→sound first, even for scripts the learner "only wants to recognise".
- **Devanagari akshara teaching (2018), *Reading and Writing*** — 35 English-speaking adults learning 80 complex akshara; copying and writing from memory built orthographic knowledge and phonological retrieval best, copying was more time-efficient, and *transparent* conjuncts (components still visible) were easier than opaque ones. [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11145-017-9767-8) — **MIXED (single lab study, well designed).** Implication: teach conjuncts by *decomposition* (क + ष → क्ष) and include tracing/handwriting, not only recognition taps.
- **Romanisation crutch:** Okuyama (2007), *CALICO Journal* 24(2) — 61 first-semester Japanese students; adding romaji to kana + audio + pictures "did not facilitate" vocabulary intake, whereas engagement with audio predicted retention. [ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ809414) — **MIXED (one study; consistent with teacher consensus).** Romanisation should be a fading scaffold (shown on first exposure, hidden by default thereafter).
- **Cross-script transfer:** Matsumoto (2013), *Modern Language Journal* 97(1) — Chinese-background beginners recognised kanji better than alphabetic-background learners, who "used a poor visual recognition strategy"; L2 exposure narrowed the gap. [ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1009581) — **STRONG for Hanzi→Kanji.** But a 2025 *Asian-Pacific J. of Second and Foreign Language Education* survey found kanji *strategy* use depends more on learning context than L1 script ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40862-025-00379-0)).
- **Script families (Brahmic, Cyrillic, Perso-Arabic):** the *Handbook of Literacy in Akshara Orthography* (Joshi & McBride, eds., Springer 2019) documents shared akshara design logic (inherent vowel, vowel matras, virama, conjuncts) and cross-language akshara-knowledge transfer in biliterate Indian children ([Google Books](https://books.google.com/books/about/Handbook_of_Literacy_in_Akshara_Orthogra.html?id=n_ecDwAAQBAJ)). **No experiment tests whether teaching adults the shared "design logic" first speeds a second Brahmic script**; the same is true for Cyrillic across Slavic and Perso-Arabic across Urdu/Persian. — **ANECDOTAL/theoretical, but mechanistically well grounded** (Taylor et al.'s generalisation result and the Matsumoto transfer effect both point the same way).
- **Time to functional literacy:** claims like "Hangul in 1–2 hours", "kana in 2–3 weeks", "Devanagari with conjuncts in 1–2 months" come from practitioner guides only. — **ANECDOTAL.** The only controlled figure is Taylor et al.'s 8 days for a 24-symbol alphabet.

## 6. Geography games, script recognition, and daily-game habits

- **Girgin (2017), *International Technology and Education Journal* 1(1)** — 103 Turkish teacher candidates played GeoGuessr 629 times; three-quarters positive; 35% said it helped course goals. Of 317 cues students reported using, **signs/plates were the largest category (30%)**, ahead of vehicles/road signs (18%), flora (17%). Qualitative, no control group. [PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED581261.pdf) — **ANECDOTAL.** No later controlled GeoGuessr/Seterra learning study with knowledge tests was found (2020–2025 literature is blog posts and lesson plans).
- **Community "meta" practice:** Plonkit ([guide](https://www.plonkit.net/guide)) and KnowYourGeo ([meta guide](https://www.knowyourgeo.com/guides/geoguessr-meta-guide/)) teach script family as a continent-level elimination filter (Cyrillic, Arabic, Thai, Devanagari, Hangul) and then finer language cues (ø/å/ä, ã/ç, ł, Thai vs Lao). This is a large informal curriculum in script/language *identification*; **no study tests transfer to language learning.** — **ANECDOTAL.**
- **Skirgård, Roberts & Yencken (2017), *PLoS ONE* 12(4)** — 15 M guesses from the Great Language Game (audio identification of 78 languages): correct identification is driven by geographic distinctiveness, speaker population, "global reach", GDP and media exposure; the authors explicitly assume casual players learn little from play. [PLOS](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0165934) — **STRONG on what makes languages recognisable; silent on learning from play.**
- **Wordle-style one-a-day (Worldle, Flagle):** designed around a single daily puzzle with progressive feedback ([Worldle description](https://studyabroad.ucmerced.edu/content/worldle-geography-themed-wordle-daily-game)); no learning-outcome studies. The supporting science is the habit literature above: a fixed daily cue at a stable time (Wood & Neal), enjoyment and self-selection (Singh et al. 2024), and tolerance for missed days (Lally 2010). — **ANECDOTAL for the games, STRONG for the habit mechanics they exploit.**

## 7. Multi-language learning and cognitive load

- **Simultaneous L2 + L3:** Huang, Steinkrauss & Verspoor (2022), *International Journal of Multilingualism* — Chinese undergraduates learning English only vs English + Russian for a year: the dual group's English did **not** develop less; the only cost was greater week-to-week *variability* in writing fluency. [ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1370864) — **MIXED (single longitudinal study).** Rahmatian & Farshadjou (2013), *International Education Studies* 6(10) — 36 Persian adults learning English + French simultaneously outperformed single-language controls, "interference" fears unsupported, motivation cited as the mechanism; small N. [PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1068706.pdf) — **MIXED.**
- **Interleaving languages within a session:** Libersky, Sobus, Slawny & Kaushanskaya (2025), *Second Language Research* — English monolinguals learning German and Polish words: interleaved beat blocked in Exp 1, but adding a mid-session break erased the difference, so the benefit is best explained by **spacing, not discriminative contrast**. [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/02676583251338768) — **MIXED.** Consistent with Brunmair & Richter (2019), *Psychological Bulletin* 145(11), 59-study meta-analysis: interleaving is large for visual categories (g ≈ 0.67), modest for maths (≈0.34), and **near zero or negative for word learning** (verify exact g). Mixing languages is fine as a *spacing* device; do not expect it to sharpen discrimination between look-alike words.
- **Similar items together:** Nakata & Suzuki (2019), *SSLA* 41(2) — 122 Japanese learners: semantically related sets produced *more interference errors* during learning and testing though equal final retention; authors advise against clustering near-synonyms. [Academia](https://www.academia.edu/36884906/) — **STRONG.** Applied to near-cognates across languages (Hindi *kitāb* / Urdu *kitāb* / Persian *ketāb* / Arabic *kitāb*): show them together for *recognition and etymology*, but test them one language at a time.
- **Cognate facilitation:** de Groot & Keijzer (2000), *Language Learning* 50(1) — cognates are learned faster and forgotten less than non-cognates; Otwinowska & Szewczyk (2019), *IJBEB* 22(8) — learning ease rises with form similarity, but *false* cognates are the costly exception. — **STRONG.** This is the empirical warrant for the word-graph/borrowing feature: it turns Indo-Aryan, Persian, Arabic and Portuguese loans into "free" vocabulary.
- **Multilingual display and cognitive load:** Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas (2019), *Educational Psychology Review* 31 — element interactivity, split attention and redundancy all rise with the number of simultaneous elements. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-019-09465-5) — **STRONG (theory + many experiments), but no study tests 4–5-language word cards directly.** EuroCom/EuroComRom "seven sieves" intercomprehension materials ([overview](https://link.springer.com/rwe/10.1007/978-3-319-02325-0_25-1)) have only programme-level qualitative evaluations. — **ANECDOTAL.**
- Duolingo's own guidance ([blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/can-you-learn-two-languages-at-the-same-time)): get one language to a foundation first, study each in a distinct time/place context, contrast false cognates explicitly, and double total time — practitioner advice aligned with the studies above.

## 8. Synthesis: evidence-ranked design principles

### (a) Culture-integrated language learning
1. **Theme-bound vocabulary recycling (narrow reading).** Keep each unit's words inside one place's geography/history/food and recycle them across dozens of micro-items. *Strength: MIXED→STRONG* (Kang 2015; Schmitt & Carter 2000; CLIL vocabulary effects in the 2023 meta-analysis).
2. **Build the cultural schema before the language input.** Flag/map/food/festival cards first, then sentences about them. *STRONG* (Steffensen et al. 1979; Erten & Razı 2009).
3. **Force deep, self-referential encoding.** "Which of these Kerala dishes would *you* order?"—one self-referent judgment per new word. *STRONG* (Symons & Johnson 1997; Pruss et al. 2025; Craik & Tulving 1975).
4. **Etymology/word-graphs as elaboration, not trivia.** Ask the learner to *guess* the origin/cognate before revealing it. *MIXED* (Boers et al. 2004; cognate facilitation is STRONG).
5. **Do not expect content-led learning to produce grammar or output.** Add explicit production tasks. *STRONG* (Goris et al. 2019; Dalton-Puffer 2011).

### (b) Micro-game / rapid-fire modes
1. **Recall before recognition.** Type/say it; MC only as a bridge. *STRONG* (Kang et al. 2007; Nakata 2016).
2. **Always show the answer** after MC/rapid items; timing of feedback matters less than its presence. *STRONG* (Butler & Roediger 2008).
3. **Speed rounds only on known items** (≥2 prior correct recalls), in Nation's fluency-strand sense; new items are untimed. *STRONG theory, MIXED direct evidence* (Nation 2007; de Jong & Perfetti 2011; Pyc & Rawson 2009).
4. **Schedule by expanding spacing, not by session count.** *STRONG* (Karatas et al. 2025 and the spacing literature).
5. **Calibrate difficulty upward**: micro-games that feel easy are teaching little. *STRONG* (Bjork & Bjork 2020).

### (c) Motivation without dark patterns
1. **Optimise the learning experience itself**; it is the strongest correlate of effort (r = .66). *STRONG* (Al-Hoorie 2018).
2. **Informational feedback and progress, not tangible rewards.** Verbal/competence feedback +0.33; expected rewards −0.36. *STRONG* (Deci et al. 1999; Sailer & Homner 2020 show motivational gains vanish in rigorous designs).
3. **Avoid public leaderboards; if social, pair competition with collaboration** (teams, shared goals). *MIXED→STRONG* (Sailer & Homner 2020; Hanus & Fox 2015; Zhang & Hasim 2023).
4. **Forgiving streaks**: two freezes at onboarding, earn-back repair, and no penalty for one missed day — a missed day does not break a habit. *STRONG habit science + large-N industry data* (Lally 2010; Singh et al. 2024; Duolingo retention team).
5. **Anchor the daily game to a stable cue** (same time, same widget); morning and self-chosen routines form faster. *STRONG* (Wood & Neal 2007; Singh et al. 2024).
6. **Measure behaviour, not stated motivation**; culture/travel intent only weakly predicts achievement. *STRONG* (Al-Hoorie 2018; Masgoret & Gardner 2003).

### (d) Script learning across families
1. **Sound-first (phonics) over shape-first.** *STRONG* (Taylor, Davis & Rastle 2017).
2. **Decompose complex graphs and include tracing/copying**, especially for conjuncts and matras. *MIXED* (Devanagari akshara study 2018).
3. **Fade romanisation quickly.** *MIXED* (Okuyama 2007).
4. **Teach the family's design logic once, then map differences** (Devanagari→Gujarati/Bengali/Gurmukhi; Hanzi→Kanji). *ANECDOTAL/theoretical*, but supported indirectly by transfer effects (Matsumoto 2013) and generalisation (Taylor et al. 2017). Instrument it: this is the app's most publishable open question.
5. **Use script identification games (GeoGuessr-style) as motivation and pre-exposure, not as literacy instruction.** *ANECDOTAL* (Girgin 2017; Plonkit).

### (e) Multi-language display
1. **Progressive disclosure in the word graph**: default to L1 + one target; expand to 4–5 equivalents on demand. *STRONG theory* (Sweller et al. 2019), *no direct test.*
2. **Show near-cognates together for meaning/etymology, test them apart.** *STRONG* (Nakata & Suzuki 2019; de Groot & Keijzer 2000).
3. **Flag false cognates explicitly** in the graph. *STRONG* (Otwinowska & Szewczyk 2019).
4. **Mixing languages in a session is safe and acts as spacing; block by language when the learner is confusing forms.** *MIXED* (Libersky et al. 2025; Brunmair & Richter 2019; Huang et al. 2022).
5. **Use pictures with translations, not instead of them**, and never let a picture inflate the learner's self-rated mastery. *STRONG* (Zhang, Sala & Gobet 2025; Carpenter & Olson 2012).

### Biggest evidence gaps worth A/B-testing in-product
- Does teaching a script family's shared logic speed the second script? (No experiment exists.)
- Do timed recognition speed-rounds add retention over untimed recall? (No direct study.)
- Do 5-language word cards hurt first-exposure learning relative to pairwise cards? (Only cognitive-load theory.)
- Does script-recognition play (GeoGuessr meta) transfer to script literacy? (Zero evidence either way.)
