# References

Every source behind Clavis, in three tiers:

1. **The in-app colophon** — 40 references the application itself cites, each reachable from the claim it supports by tapping a `[n]` superscript. This is the curated set: the claims the app actually makes on screen.
2. **The full research bibliography** — 473 unique URLs across seven research memos, grouped by memo. Entry counts per memo are slightly higher, because a source cited in two memos is listed in both.
3. **Data sources and licences** — what the app is built out of, and under what terms.

A note on verification, repeated from `03-evidence.md` §0 because it governs how this list should be read: **not every source here was read from the primary text.** The memos carry their own provenance flags and those flags are preserved below. Two of the seven memos ran out of search budget part-way through, so the unverified sources cluster rather than scattering. Anything marked `NOT VERIFIED`, `[unverified]`, `[K]` or "secondary summary" should be checked against the primary before being quoted outside this project. The consolidated do-not-quote list is in `03-evidence.md` §13.

## Part 1 — The in-app colophon (40 sources)

These are the references the shipping app links to. Each entry shows the source, the paper, the URL, and the one-line gloss the app displays — which is written to say *what the app does about it*, not merely what the paper found.

1. **Nation 2006** — *How large a vocabulary is needed for reading and listening?*  
   <https://www.scienceguide.nl/wp-content/uploads/2017/11/nation-2006-vocabulary.pdf>  
   2,000 word families cover ~89% of conversation; 98% coverage needs 6,000–9,000.
2. **Cepeda et al. 2008** — *Spacing effects in learning: a temporal ridgeline of optimal retention*  
   <https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED505660.pdf>  
   1,354 people; recall 64% higher at the optimal gap; the gap grows with how long you want to remember.
3. **Karpicke & Roediger 2008** — *The critical importance of retrieval for learning*  
   <https://web.mit.edu/jbelcher/www/learner/retrieval.pdf>  
   Tested learners recalled ~80% a week later; re-studiers 33–36%.
4. **Webb 2007** — *The effects of repetition on vocabulary knowledge*  
   <https://academic.oup.com/applij/article-abstract/28/1/46/174744>  
   ~10 encounters is the working dose for incidental word learning.
5. **Schmidt 1990** — *The role of consciousness in second language learning*  
   <https://academic.oup.com/applij/article-abstract/11/2/129/163482>  
   "Subliminal language learning is impossible" — attention is required; Clavis never claims sleep-learning.
6. **Gooskens et al. 2017** — *Mutual intelligibility between closely related languages in Europe*  
   <https://www.let.rug.nl/gooskens/pdf/publ_ijm_2017.pdf>  
   Untrained listeners: Portuguese→Spanish 77%, Spanish→Portuguese 37%; reception is cheap, and asymmetric.
7. **Lotto & de Groot 1998** — *Effects of learning method and word type on acquiring vocabulary*  
   <https://www.academia.edu/12744827/>  
   Cognates recalled 86.5% vs 72% for non-cognates.
8. **Rothman 2011** — *The Typological Primacy Model*  
   <https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0267658310386439>  
   Transfer comes from the language you perceive as closest, not your first.
9. **Carvalho & da Silva 2006** — *Cross-linguistic influence in third language acquisition*  
   <https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ741850>  
   Producing two sibling languages early breeds blends (portuñol); Clavis widens reading, narrows speaking.
10. **Clement, Roy, Oudeyer & Lopes 2015** — *Multi-armed bandits for intelligent tutoring systems*  
   <https://arxiv.org/abs/1310.3174>  
   ZPDES: pick the next activity by learning progress, per learner, no population data — the engine behind Paths.
11. **Doignon & Falmagne 1985–2012** — *Knowledge space theory*  
   <https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-3-642-58625-5>  
   What you know is a state in a prerequisite structure; the "fringe" is what you are ready to learn next.
12. **Open Spaced Repetition 2023–26** — *FSRS benchmark (~350M reviews)*  
   <https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/srs-benchmark>  
   FSRS beats SM-2 in ~99.6% of collections; Clavis schedules every word with FSRS-5.
13. **Nakata & Suzuki 2019** — *Mixing semantically related words hurts*  
   <https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/effects-of-massing-and-spacing-on-the-learning-of-semantically-related-and-unrelated-words/F58BA8D70385603B9C42E408BFCB8A10>  
   Days-of-the-week-style sets interfere ~3× more; Clavis never introduces them together.
14. **Yonelinas 2002** — *The nature of recollection and familiarity*  
   <https://doi.org/10.1006/jmla.2002.2864>  
   Familiarity without recollection is real memory signal — the "tingle."
15. **Nelson 1978** — *Savings in relearning*  
   <https://psycnet.apa.org/doi/10.1037/0278-7393.4.5.453>  
   Words you can neither recall nor recognise are still relearned faster than new ones.
16. **Bowers, Mattys & Gage 2009** — *Preserved implicit knowledge of a forgotten childhood language*  
   <https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2009.02407.x>  
   Adults relearned "lost" Hindi/Zulu phonemes better than controls, with no explicit memory.
17. **Frances, Martín & Duñabeitia 2020** — *The effects of contextual diversity on word learning*  
   <https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-020-70922-1>  
   Same 8 exposures across more texts → better recall, recognition and meaning — including in a foreign language.
18. **Rosa, Tapia & Perea 2017** — *Contextual diversity in children's word learning*  
   <https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0179004>  
   Words met across genres beat words met in one, on every measure.
19. **Adelman, Brown & Quesada 2006** — *Contextual diversity, not word frequency, determines word-naming and lexical decision times*  
   <https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01787.x>  
   The count that matters is distinct contexts, not raw repetitions.
20. **Ludke, Ferreira & Overy 2014** — *Singing can facilitate foreign language learning*  
   <https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13421-013-0342-5>  
   Singing beat speaking for verbatim phrase production (d ≈ 0.75).
21. **Wallace 1994** — *Memory for music: effect of melody on recall of text*  
   <https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bbd36596cf39f88c1f3066526a96561b4768f484>  
   Melody helps only when simple and repeated; a per-verse melody makes recall worse than speech.
22. **Pavia, Webb & Faez 2019** — *Incidental vocabulary learning through listening to songs*  
   <https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/abs/incidental-vocabulary-learning-through-listening-to-songs/24418DF071558AE40C2D429B6868F8B9>  
   Song listening teaches word forms; gains scale with repetitions.
23. **Souza & Barbosa 2023** — *Should we turn off the music?*  
   <https://journalofcognition.org/articles/10.5334/joc.273>  
   Background music with lyrics impairs verbal memory and reading (d ≈ −0.3); Clavis plays nothing behind exercises.
24. **Sundararajan & Adesope 2020** — *Keep it coherent: seductive details meta-analysis*  
   <https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-020-09522-4>  
   Interesting-but-irrelevant media reliably hurts learning; every picture here depicts the phrase.
25. **Barcroft 2002** — *Semantic and structural elaboration in L2 lexical acquisition*  
   <https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ647300>  
   Deep meaning-work at first exposure suppresses learning the word form — so origins unlock on the second meeting.
26. **Boers, Eyckmans & Stengers 2007** — *Presenting figurative idioms with etymology*  
   <https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1362168806072460>  
   Knowing where an expression comes from aids inference and retention.
27. **Bahrick, Bahrick, Bahrick & Bahrick 1993** — *Maintenance of foreign language vocabulary and the spacing effect*  
   <https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-9280.1993.tb00571.x>  
   13 sessions 56 days apart preserved vocabulary like 26 sessions 14 days apart — maintenance is cheap.
28. **Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999** — *Meta-analysis of extrinsic rewards and intrinsic motivation*  
   <https://psycnet.apa.org/doi/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627>  
   Expected tangible rewards undermine intrinsic motivation; informational feedback strengthens it. No XP here.
29. **Lally et al. 2010** — *How are habits formed*  
   <https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ejsp.674>  
   Median 66 days to automaticity; missing one day doesn't dent the curve — streak guilt is not evidence-based.
30. **Kang, McDermott & Roediger 2007** — *Test format and corrective feedback*  
   <https://doi.org/10.1080/09541440601056620>  
   Short-answer practice beats multiple choice for later retention — recall before recognition.
31. **Sarma 2025** — *Heritage, multilingualism and education in India (IIT Bombay, n = 1,375)*  
   <https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2025.1538482/full>  
   English-medium graduates keep spoken heritage languages but cede reading and formal register — why the baseline tests exist.
32. **Fourrier & Sagot 2020** — *EtymDB 2.0: a large etymological database from Wiktionary*  
   <https://aclanthology.org/2020.lrec-1.397/>  
   Source of the real inheritance/borrowing/cognacy links in the Cosmos (CC BY-SA 4.0).
33. **Skirgård, Roberts & Yencken 2017** — *Why are some languages confused for others? Investigating data from the Great Language Game*  
   <https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0165934>  
   Nearly 15 million guesses from over a million players in 80+ countries: a language is confused with its geographic neighbours more often than with its genealogical relatives.
34. **Girgin 2017** — *Cues used by teacher candidates to locate places in GeoGuessr*  
   <https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED581261.pdf>  
   Among 103 participants, written signs were the single largest cue category (~30%) for placing an unfamiliar scene — script is a geographic signal.
35. **Brady, Konkle, Alvarez & Oliva 2008** — *Visual long-term memory has a massive storage capacity for object details*  
   <https://www.pnas.org/doi/abs/10.1073/pnas.0803390105>  
   After seeing 2,500 objects once, people recognised them at 87–92% — a glimpse leaves far more trace than it feels like.
36. **Furnas 1986** — *Generalized fisheye views*  
   <https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/22627.22342>  
   Degree-of-interest views: magnify what is in focus while keeping the surroundings in sight — the idea behind the Cosmos fisheye.
37. **Sarkar & Brown 1992** — *Graphical fisheye views of graphs*  
   <https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/142750.142763>  
   The distortion behind the Explore fisheye: points near the lens centre spread apart, distant ones compress — and the space gained is spent on labels. Full report: bitsavers.org/pdf/dec/tech_reports/SRC-RR-84A.pdf
38. **Tominski, Gladisch, Kister, Dachselt & Schumann 2017** — *Interactive lenses for visualization: an extended survey*  
   <https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/cgf.12871>  
   Survey of lens techniques including the local edge filters the Relations lens implements.
39. **Collins, Penn & Carpendale 2009** — *Bubble Sets: revealing set relations with isocontours over existing visualizations*  
   <https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/5290706/>  
   Set membership drawn as smooth isocontours over a point cloud — the Families outline and the Paths groupings, drawn by the BubbleSets algorithm itself (via AntV G6).
40. **Fruchterman & Reingold 1991** — *Graph drawing by force-directed placement*  
   <https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/spe.4380211102>  
   Springs pull related nodes together, repulsion pushes all apart — the layout used for a word's web, and (as d3-force) for the Paths map.

### The licensing rule

Content packs are restricted to **CC0 / CC BY / CC BY-SA** so a paid tier stays legally possible. The architecture that makes this work: a **CC BY-SA data pack alongside own-licence application code**, with commercial use permitted and no contamination between the two.

**Excluded on NC grounds, with the cost recorded:** CogNet, PanLex, MMS-TTS, NLLB. This is not a free win — excluding MMS-TTS means **Kannada and Konkani have no offline permissive TTS voice at all**, which the research names directly as a consequence.

**Licences marked "must-check-before-shipping"** in memo 05, none of which are load-bearing in the current build but all of which would be if the project expands: Etymological Wordnet (CC BY-SA 3.0?), Hermit Dave FrequencyWords, Leipzig corpora, NorthEuraLex, KartaView, Panoramax, and **every individual Piper TTS voice** (some are CC BY-NC).

**Not mentioned in any memo**, so their inclusion or exclusion cannot be justified by reference to this research: `xkcd`, `NLLB`, `Wikinews`, `Global Voices`.
